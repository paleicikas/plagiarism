Individual differences in self-efficacy development: The effects of goal orientation and affectivity
Introduction
Over the last two decades self-efficacy has emerged as an important motivational construct in the study of human behavior, particularly in work settings (Bandura & Locke, 2003, Gist, 1987, Gist & Mitchell, 1992, Stajkovic & Luthans, 1998). Self-efficacy is defined as "an individual's belief in one's capability to organize and execute the courses of action required to produce given attainments" (Bandura, 1997, p. 3). High levels of self-efficacy have been found to lead to increased levels of performance across a variety of tasks (Bandura, 1997, Frayne & Geringer, 1994, Stajkovic & Luthans, 1998).
The self-efficacy construct is derived from social cognitive theory, which states that human functioning is a result of interplay between personal, behavioral, and environmental influences (Bandura, 1986, Pajares, 2002). Social cognitive theory argues for the importance of human agency, viewing an individual as being influential in his or her own development. In keeping with this view, an individual is able to exercise control over his or her own thoughts, feelings, and actions, and this control is heavily influenced by an individual's view of self.
When viewing self-efficacy in terms of human agency, two salient arguments emerge: 1) self-efficacy is malleable, it can be changed and developed; and 2) given the important influential role of the individual's view of self in agency theory, individual differences should impact self-efficacy development. To date, there is research and support for the malleability of efficacy (Bandura, 1997), as well as research on the relationship between individual differences and efficacy at one point in time (Phillips & Gully, 1997, Treasure et al., 1996), but to date, research on the impact of individual differences on efficacy development has been virtually non-existent (see Martocchio, 1994, Mathieu et al., 1993 for exceptions).
Given the importance social cognitive theory places on an individual's cognitions and views of self, it seems logical that certain individual differences that impact the way an individual views his or herself and the world should play an important role in efficacy development. The current study aims to explore the effect of two individual difference constructs on self-efficacy development: goal orientation and affectivity. Gist and Mitchell (1992) stated that two internal, individual difference factors affect the development of self-efficacy: 1) whether an individual sees his or her ability as fixed or malleable, and 2) the nature of the individual's arousal (either positive or negative). In keeping with this framework, we argue that the constructs of goal orientation and affectivity capture these two factors identified and thus should be examined.
Past research has found both of these individual differences to be related to views of self and to the efficacy construct at one given point in time (Ford et al., 1998, Kozlowski et al., 2001), yet to date no studies have looked specifically at the impact these constructs have on the development of self-efficacy. Below we review what is known about self-efficacy development, and discuss the theoretical rationale for why we believe these individual differences of goal orientation and affectivity should logically impact the development of efficacy.
Theory suggests that task-specific self-efficacy is highly malleable (Bandura, 1997) and research supports this assertion (e.g. Frayne & Latham, 1987, Martocchio, 1994, Mathieu et al., 1993). More specifically, past studies have shown increases in self-efficacy following a variety of experiences, including behavioral modeling situations (Gist, Schwoerer, & Rosen, 1989), and by verbal persuasion techniques (Eden & Zuk, 1995). In still other studies, task-specific efficacy has been increased by participation in enactive mastery experiences, such as self-management training (Frayne & Latham, 1987). This characteristic of efficacy makes it appealing to managers, who may be able to increase employees' self-efficacy levels, and subsequently, their job performance.
Bandura (1997) argues that self-efficacy can develop through four sources: enactive mastery, verbal persuasion, vicarious modeling, and physiological arousal. Enactive mastery is the most influential source of efficacy information, and as such it is the focus of the current study. Enactive mastery allows an individual to acquire cognitive, affective, and behavioral tools that lead to a belief that one can effectively accomplish one's goals (Bandura, 1997). Typically enactive mastery experiences provide individuals with knowledge and strategies to pursue effective courses of behavior. Past research has often used training as an enactive mastery experience (e.g. Frayne & Latham, 1987, Gist et al., 1989, Tannenbaum et al., 1991).
While enactive mastery has powerful effects on efficacy development, we know little about the degree to which individual differences influence the extent of efficacy development from such experiences. While a number of studies have now demonstrated moderate to large correlations between trainee disposition and self-efficacy (e.g., Kozlowski et al., 2001, Martocchio & Judge, 1997, Treasure et al., 1996), this research typically examines effects of disposition on pre-training or post-training self-efficacy, not on change in self-efficacy from pre- to post-training. Thus, the conclusions of these studies do not fully address the question of individual difference effects on self-efficacy development.
In this section, we discuss both the theoretical rationale for the effect of goal orientation on self-efficacy development and prior research which supports this rationale. Self-efficacy, by definition, is a judgment made by an individual about his/her capabilities to achieve future success (Gist & Mitchell, 1992). According to Bandura (1997), "Appraisal of personal efficacy is an inferential process in which factors must be weighed. The extent to which people will alter their perceived efficacy will depend on (among other factors) their preconceptions of their abilities..." (p. 81). We argue that this preconception of ability is captured in part by the individual difference of goal orientation, a position that is well-supported by other researchers (e.g. Cron et al., 2005, Robins & Pals, 2003). Goal orientation involves the approach that individuals' adopt to achievement situations, and is rooted in personal beliefs about whether abilities are fixed or malleable (Dweck, 1986). Goal orientations represent different perceptual frameworks that influence cognitive, affect, and behavior.
Early research on goal orientation explored the concept that there was only one dimension of goal orientation, and that mastery and performance goal orientation were simply ends of a single continuum (Dweck, 1986). However, more recent research has established that mastery and performance goal orientation are instead independent dimensions (Ames & Archer, 1988, Button et al., 1996, Colquitt & Simmering, 1998, Heyman & Dweck, 1992, Phillips & Gully, 1997). Mastery goal orientation involves a preference for goals that involve learning new skills and mastering unfamiliar situations. Mastery goal orientation leads to an adaptive behavior pattern, wherein an individual seeks challenging tasks and continues to persist toward mastery of those tasks, even in the face of obstacles (Dweck, 1986, Dweck & Leggett, 1988). In contrast, a performance goal orientation involves a preference for goals that allow an individual to display his or her competence and receive favorable judgments about their performance (Dweck, 1986). Individuals with a performance goal orientation avoid challenge, and resist the pursuit of goals in the face of obstacles.
MGO and PGO are based on differing theories and viewpoints of abilities and skills (Cron et al., 2005). Following Dweck's (1986) theory, individuals with a MGO have been found to be subscribe to an incremental implicit theory (Robins & Pals, 2003), meaning those with a mastery goal orientation believe their abilities and skills are malleable. Holding such a view about oneself leads the individual to consequently be oriented to set goals to master new skills and material because he or she believes such mastery is possible through effort and persistence. Past research has supported the positive relationship between MGO and self-efficacy at one single point in time (Brown, 2001, Phillips & Gully, 1997).
In terms of efficacy development, the belief that competence will come through work and persistence should be conducive to developing higher self-efficacy as a result of enactive mastery experiences, because of the opportunities such experiences provide for practice and mastery of skills and material. Martocchio (1994), for example, found that individuals who believed they could acquire a skill through training experienced a greater increase in self-efficacy than those who believed their abilities were fixed.
Therefore, it is our assertion that individuals who are high in mastery goal orientation will experience a greater increase in self-efficacy when exposed to an enactive mastery training experience than individuals lower in mastery goal orientation. H1 Trainees with high MGO will experience a greater increase in self-efficacy as a result of training than trainees with low MGO.
Individuals high in PGO, in contrast, subscribe to an entity implicit theory (Dweck, 1986, Robins & Pals, 2003). These individuals believe their abilities and skills are fixed; that is, that no amount of effort or persistence on their part will lead to mastery of new skills or an improvement of abilities. Because these individuals view their ability as a fixed entity, they tend to set goals where they feel they can readily demonstrate and document, rather than develop, competence. They also seek goals which will allow them to readily receive positive feedback, and avoid negative feedback (Dweck & Leggett, 1988). Such goals are typically maladaptive, because challenges exist in most important pursuits. Having a view of self that discourages individuals from persisting in the face of obstacles, or from functioning effectively when confronting difficulties, limits what an individual will accomplish (Dweck & Leggett, 1988).
Past research has found no relationship, or a small negative relationship, between PGO and self-efficacy (Brown, 2001, Phillips & Gully, 1997). In terms of self-efficacy development, holding a view of self that suggests no amount of practice or learning will lead to increased competence should lead to little development in efficacy as a result of an enactive mastery experience. We argue that individuals higher in performance goal orientation will experience less of an increase in self-efficacy as a result of a training experience than those lower in performance goal orientation. H2 Trainees with low PGO will experience a greater increase in self-efficacy as a result of training than trainees with high PGO.
Research on affectivity in organizational behavior has recently experienced a "veritable explosion" (Ashkinasay, 2004, p.137). Erez and Isen (2002) called for more studies examining the role affectivity may play in modern motivational theories, such as self-efficacy. In the following section, we discuss the rationale for the examination of the effects of affectivity on efficacy development.
Bandura, 1986, Bandura, 1997 has long cited physiological arousal as one of the primary sources of self-efficacy. When individuals experience negative or positive feelings and emotions, they impact self-efficacy perceptions (Pajares, 2002). Individuals rely partly on information from their bodies' physiological messages to assess their capabilities. For example, if an individual feels anxious about a task, he or she is likely to assess that he/she does not possess the capability to perform that task. Dispositional affectivity is the typical level of physiological arousal or activation of an individual, and indicates whether an individual is prone to positive emotions and moods (positive affectivity) or negative emotions and moods (negative affectivity; Watson, Clark, & Tellegen, 1988).
Positive affectivity (PA) refers to the extent to which a person is generally alert, active, and enthusiastic. Individuals high in PA are more likely to exhibit positive mood states, and thus be high in energy and concentration (Watson et al., 1988). Past research has shown that positive mood leads to higher levels of motivation and that positive physiological arousal has a facilitative effect on motivation (Erez & Isen, 2002). Dispositional positive affectivity has been found to lead to better adaptation, and the emphasis on positive aspects of challenging situations (Johnson & Tversky, 1983, Salovey & Birnbaum, 1989, Schwarz & Bohner, 1996, Wegener et al., 1994). Individuals high in positive affectivity are also able to direct their energies more appropriately than those lower in positive affectivity, leading to flexibility, creativity and persistence (Scheier, Weintraub, & Carver, 1986).
In keeping with this logic, previous research has found positive affectivity to be positively related to self-efficacy at one point in time (Mullins et al., 1998, Tate et al., 1995, Treasure et al., 1996), with specific findings that positive affectivity may facilitate efficacy judgments (Staw & Barsade, 1993, Wright & Staw, 1999). In terms of efficacy development, we argue that individuals high in PA will experience high levels of positive arousal during an enactive mastery training experience, and will approach such an experience with energy and engagement, as well as a higher expectancy that efforts during this experience will be worthwhile. Therefore, we predict that high PA individuals will experience greater self-efficacy development as compared to those lower in PA. H3 Trainees with high PA will experience a greater increase in self-efficacy as a result of training than trainees with low PA.
Negative affect refers to the degree of distress and displeasurable engagement. Individuals high in negative affectivity (NA) are prone to experience high levels of anger, guilt, disgust, and fear. Those low in NA are, in contrast, calm and serene (Watson et al., 1988). Past research has found a negative relationship between negative affect and self-efficacy (Treasure et al., 1996), and it has been found that negative thoughts and feelings lower efficacy perceptions (Pajares, 2002).
Conceptually, NA should hinder the development of efficacy. Negative moods activate a global view of one-self as inadequate and worthless (Teasdale, 1988). Negative affectivity is also associated with denial, disengagement from goals, and a focus on stressful feelings (Scheier et al., 1986). In the context of an enactive mastery training experience, individuals high in negative arousal are likely to interpret any difficulties or challenges they encounter during training as signs that they are incapable of successfully mastering the material. As a result, those high in NA will likely shy away from the material. In contrast, those lower in NA will not experience the inhibiting effects of negative arousal during training, and should be more likely to develop self-efficacy over the course of the training program. H4 Trainees with low NA will experience a greater increase in self-efficacy as a result of training than trainees with high NA.
