Longevity, Big Five Personality Factors,
and Health Behaviors:
Presidents From Washington to Nixon
STEWART J. H. MCCANN
Department of Psychology
Cape Breton University, Canada
ABSTRACT. In this study, the positive correlation between conscientiousness and death
age discovered by H. S. Friedman et al. (1993, 1995) was replicated for 32 American presidents. Conscientiousness scores, using the Big Five variables produced by S. J. Rubenzer,
T. R. Faschingbauer, and D. S. Ones (1996, 2000), were correlated with death age, K30) =
.41, /? < .01, and the relationship was maintained with controls in a multiple regression
framework. Smoking, drinking, and exercise, as assessed by J. R. Bumgamer (1994), were
also correlated in the expected fashion with death age. Conscientiousness was correlated
negatively with smoking and drinking, but openness to experience was correlated negatively with smoking and drinking to a higher degree and also was positively correlated with
exercise. Openness, extraversion, neuroticism, and agreeableness were not correlated with
death age, and extraversion, neuroticism, and agreeableness were not correlated with smoking, drinking or exercise.
Key words: alcohol. Big Five, conscientiousness, exercise, health, openness, personality,
presidents, smoking
A POSITIVE CORRELATION BETWEEN LONGEVITY and conscientiousness measured during childhood became apparent when Friedman and his associates (e.g., Friedman et al., 1995; Friedman et al., 1993) analyzed data from the
large-scale longitudinal Terman Life-Cycle Study of more than 1,500 bright California students initiated in 1921 (e.g., Terman & Oden, 1947). The results of this
prospective study also showed that the size of the conscientiousness-longevity
relationship was relatively large, "comparable to the effects of systolic blood pressure or serum cholesterol on all-cause mortality" (Friedman et al., 1995, p. 697).
In addition, Friedman et al. (1995) found that conscientiousness was related
to smoking and drinking and that smoking and drinking were related to longevity.

Furthermore, survival analysis showed that the positive correlation between
longevity and conscientiousness remained significant when the effects of smoking
and drinking were statistically controlled, and that conscientiousness, smoking,
and drinking each accounted for independent portions of the variance in death age.
Apparently, no one has reported an attempt to replicate the conscientiousness-longevity link established by Friedman and his associates. In tangentially
related research, however, researchers have found conscientiousness to be associated with feelings of well-being (McRae & Costa, 1991; Watson & Clark,
1992) and with perceptions of good health (Goodwin & Engstrom, 2002). Others have established connections between lower conscientiousness and smoking
(Gilbert & Gilbert, 1995; Kubicka, Matejcek, Dytrych, & Roth, 2001; Presson,
Chassin, & Sherman, 2002) and drinking (Cook, Young, Taylor, & Bedford,
1998; Kubicka et al.; Loukas, Krull, Chassin, & Carle, 2000; Stewart, Loughlin,
&Rhyno, 2001).
Other researchers have also found a shorter life span to be associated with
smoking (Cockerham, 2000; Paffenbarger, Hyde, Wing, & Hsieh, 1986; Paffenbarger et al., 1994; Pearl, 1938) and drinking (Cockerham; Fillmore, Kerr, &
Bostrom, 2003; Pearl), although it has been generally recognized for a long time
that heavy drinking shortens life, whereas moderate drinking and total abstention
do not (e.g., Fillmore et al.; Pearl).
I conducted the present study to determine whether the major findings of
Friedman et al. (1995) could be replicated and extended with data for a relatively
small but eminent sample (Simonton, 1999, 2003), the 32 U.S. presidents from
Washington to Nixon who were not assassinated. Assessments of the presidents
by Rubenzer et al., (1996, 2000) provided scores for what are commonly referred
to as the Big Five personality factors (e.g., McCrae et al., 2000): Conscientiousness, Openness to Experience, Extraversion, Neuroticism, and Agreeableness.
Information about the smoking, drinking, and exercise behavior of the presidents
was available from Bumgarner's (1994) book on the health of the presidents.
Although Friedman et al. (1993, 1995) did not include exercise as behavior
related to health, it was included in the present study because researchers have
found conscientiousness to be positively correlated with exercise (Coumeya,
Bobick, & Schinke, 1999; Coumeya & Hellsten, 1998), and exercise to be related
to a longer life span (Cockerham, 2000; Kahana et al., 2002; Paffenbarger et al.,
1986; Paffenbarger et al. 1994).
On the basis of the theory and findings of Friedman et al. (1993, 1995), I
made the following predictions:
1. Conscientiousness of the presidents would be positively correlated with
death age;
2. conscientiousness would be negatively correlated with smoking;
3. conscientiousness would be negatively correlated with drinking;
4. conscientiousness would be positively correlated with exercise;

5. smoking would be negatively correlated with death age;
6. drinking would be negatively correlated with death age; and
7. exercise would be positively correlated with death age.
In addition, I planned analyses to explore further the relationships between openness to experience, extraversion, neuroticism, and agreeableness to death age,

Method
Sample
All of the 32 deceased presidents from Washington to Nixon who were not
assassinated (i.e., Lincoln, Garfield, McKinley, Kennedy) were included in the
sample.
Measures
Death age, inauguration age, and birth year. For each president, death age in
years (to one decimal place) was taken by age at death (2003) on March 23,
2003, inauguration age in years (to one decimal place) was taken by the age at
which they took office (2003) on October 3, 2003, and the birth year was taken
by birthday (2003) on October 3, 2003.1 included the inauguration age and birth
year primarily so that they could be used as variables under statistical control
while testing the predictions. Inauguration age is predictive of death age (see
McCann, 2001, 2003), and I expected that birth year might account for timerelated changes, especially in the health behaviors.
Big Five personality variables. Rubenzer et al. (1996) had panels of experts rate
each of the presidents using three assessment instruments: the Revised NEO Personality Inventory (Costa & McCrae, 1992), the 100 Adjective Clusters (Goldberg, 1990), and the California Q-Sort (Block, 1978). Each of the three instruments was scored to produce values on each of the Big Eive personality
variables. Reliability and validity information is provided by Rubenzer et al.
(1996,2000).
Rubenzer et al. (1996) reported two scores for conscientiousness, openness
to experience, extraversion, neuroticism, and agreeableness. One score was based
purely on the Revised NEO Personality Inventory, and the other was a total score
based on the unweighted combination of the assessments provided by the Revised
NEO Personality Inventory, the 100 Adjective Clusters, and the California Q-Sort.
For the present research purposes, I converted the two scores reported by Rubenzer et al. (1996) for the 36 presidents from Washington to Nixon to z scores, added
them together, and converted the sums to z scores.

Each of these final z scores, which represented conscientiousness, openness
to experience, extraversion, neuroticism, and agreeableness, was therefore a
weighted combination of the Revised NEO Personality Inventory, the 100 Adjective Clusters, and the California Q-Sort assessments, wherein the resulting score
was two thirds based on the Revised NEO Personality Inventory, one sixth based
on the 100 Adjective Clusters, and one sixth based on the California Q-Sort. This
variable construction approach used all of the available information in the
Rubenzer et al. (1996) report, but also maintained the emphasis on the widely
popular Revised NEO Personality Inventory, which also was highlighted in the
Rubenzer et al. (2000) article.
Health practices. Bumgarner (1994), a physician, has provided a relatively
detailed account of the health-related problems and practices of each of the presidents. Of course, determining health practices and problems of the presidents,
especially the earlier ones, is a considerable challenge. So, Bumgarner's information might not always represent the comprehensive reality of a president; nevertheless, it seemed to be the best single account available. Therefore, with due
caution, I used Bumgarner's book to form the smoking, drinking, and exercise
variables.
According to Bumgarner (1994), the presidents who used tobacco were
Jackson, Taylor, Grant, Arthur, Cleveland, B. Harrison, Harding, Coolidge,
Eisenhower, and L. Johnson. They were given a value of 1 and all others were
given a value of 0 on the dichotomous smoking variable. Taylor chewed tobacco; several presidents regularly smoked cigars (Cleveland, B. Harrison, and
Coolidge), and Grant chewed tobacco and was also a heavy cigar smoker. Apparently, Hoover "learned to enjoy a good cigar" in college but "later would not
allow himself to be photographed smoking a cigar" (Bumgarner, p. 201). Hoover
was given a value of 0 on the smoking variable because there is no indication that
he was a habitual user of tobacco, as were all of the others placed in the "smoking" category.
Those who used alcohol to excess occasionally or habitually were Van
Buren, Pierce, Buchanan, A. Johnson, Grant, Arthur, Cleveland, Harding, L.
Johnson, and Nixon. They were given a value of 1 and all others were given a
value of 0 on the drinking variable. Those who took a social drink or could be
classified as only moderate drinkers were excluded from the drinking category.
For instance, T. Roosevelt was excluded even though he "consumed a moderate
amount of wine and occasionally . . . mint juleps" (Bumgarner, 1994, p. 160).
Also excluded were Hoover, who "took an occasional social drink" (Bumgarner,
p. 201); Truman, who "drank moderately" (Bumgarner, p. 221); and Eisenhower, who was a moderate and careful social drinker (Bumgarner, p. 228). Jefferson, who drank three glasses of very weak wine a day and had malt liquor and
cider at his meals also was excluded because, of course, there was no indication
that he used alcohol to excess occasionally or habitually. He was an ardent advo-

The presidents classified as engaging in exercise were J. Adams, Jefferson,
J. Q. Adams, Hayes, B. Harrison, T. Roosevelt, Taft, Harding, Hoover, E. Roosevelt, Truman, Eisenhower, and Nixon. They were given a value of 1 and all

1 month later, tried to begin each day of his extremely short presidency with a
morning walk (Bumgarner, 1994, p. 61), but he was not included as an exerciser because there was no evidence that he engaged in regular exercise during his
adult life.
Arthur had a notably sedentary lifestyle (Bumgarner, 1994, p. 131) but during his presidency "almost daily sought to escape the stress of his office by walking and horseback riding" (Bumgarner, p. 133). On the basis of Bumgarner's
account of Arthur's motivation and his generally sedentary behavior, he too was
excluded from the exercise category. The placing of F. Roosevelt presented
somewhat of a dilemma. Although he was an early victim of polio, he was
included as an exerciser because of his dedication over many years to the
strengthening of his muscles to compensate for his disability as much as possible (Bumgarner, p. 210), coupled with the athletic dedication and desire that he
displayed in his years at Harvard. (As it turned out, however, including E. Roosevelt as an exerciser actually worked against finding support for the exercise
hypotheses of the study because he had a conscientiousness z score of-1.49 and
died at the relatively young age of 63.2.).
Procedure
I tested the seven predictions with correlations, using one-tailed significance
tests with alpha levels of .05. In all other analyses, I used two-tailed significance
tests with alpha levels of .05. Unless otherwise stated, significance tests were
two-tailed.
Planned exploratory analyses included the following: (a) correlations to
determine the associations among the Big Eive personality variables and among
the three health behaviors; (b) correlations to determine whether any of the other
Big Eive personality variables were related to death age or the health behaviors;
(c) multiple regression analysis to determine which of the Big Five personality
dimensions accounted for variance in death age when birth year, inauguration
age, smoking, drinking, and exercise were statistically controlled; and (d) logistic regression to determine which, if any, of the Big Five variables predicted
smoking, drinking, and exercise.

Although a preceding analysis showed that only one of the Big Five variables-conscientiousness-was significantly correlated with death age, the question now arose as to whether intellectual brilliance as assessed by Simonton (1986)
or intelligence as assessed by Rubenzer et al. (1996) was related to death age. I

Discussion
The main finding of Friedman et al. (1995) was replicated in the present
study. For the 32 presidents from Washington to Nixon used in the present study,
conscientiousness was positively correlated with length of life. The positive correlation between conscientiousness and length of life was also maintained when
year of birth, inauguration age, smoking, drinking, and exercise were statistically
controlled in a multiple regression. The correlation between conscientiousness
and longevity also showed transhistorical stability.
In line with the other results of Friedman et al. (1995), conscientiousness
was negatively correlated with smoking and drinking, and death age was negatively correlated with smoking and drinking (at the .10 level). However, unlike
the results of Friedman et al., multiple regression strategies revealed that smoking and drinking did not contribute independently above and beyond conscientiousness to the variance accounted for in death age.
Results for exercise, the third health behavior, which was not included in the
Friedman et al. (1995) research, were mixed. The positive correlation between
conscientiousness and exercise was not significant. However, exercise was positively correlated with death age, and exercise did account for significant additional variance in death age even when it was preceded in the regression equation
by conscientiousness.
Generally, the findings of Friedman et al. (1995) were replicated and extended
even though the present sample was small and somewhat unrepresentative of the
general populace. Sample size was necessarily restricted to 32 and the ratio of cases
to predictors was much lower than desirable for multiple regression analysis. Nevertheless, the variance in death age accounted for by conscientiousness was only
marginally less when adjusted for attenuation when birth year and inauguration age
were controlled (13.4% vs. 11.9%), and when the three health variables were also
controlled (9.7% vs. 9.4%).
As well, the sample was even less representative of the American population
than the Terman gifted sample (Terman & Oden, 1947) on which the Friedman

et al. (1995) results were based. American presidents surely form an elite group
in numerous respects, and they also differ specifically on key variables used in
this study. Rubenzer et al. (1996, p. 4) concluded that they are not so different
from the average American today, but also reported (p. 408) that the average
president was rated somewhat higher on neuroticism, extraversion, and conscientiousness and somewhat lower on openness to experience and agreeableness.
The respective T scores were 52, 55, 56, 45, and 43.
Yet, despite such sample deficiencies, the positive correlation between conscientiousness and death age and most of the other relationships found by Friedman et al. (1995) clearly were confirmed, thereby boosting confidence in the
robustness of the conscientiousness-death age link and the importance of conscientiousness in regard to health behaviors. Of course, there were sample advantages too. The large body of information concerning the presidents provided a
conveniently accessible source of relevant data, which provided an opportunity
not only to replicate earlier findings using presidents as "proxies for human
beings at large" (Simonton, 1994, p. 432), but to generalize earlier findings to a
truly extraordinary sample (see Simonton, pp. 432-433).
Because the present study included all of the Big Five personality variables
rather than only conscientiousness, as in the Friedman et al. (1995) research, I
was able to determine whether other Big Five variables also were related to death
age or to health behaviors. There was no evidence that any other Big Five variable was related to death age, but I found that openness to experience was correlated with smoking, drinking, and exercise (at the .10 level). Logistic regression also showed that openness to experience rather than conscientiousness was
the prime variable in relation to smoking, drinking, and exercise.
The relationship between openness to experience and health behaviors was
generalized to the intelligence assessment of the presidents by Rubenzer et al.
(1996, 2000) and to the intellectual brilliance variable of Simonton (1986).
However, the three measures were not conceptually isomorphic even though
they were correlated to a substantial degree and produced fairly parallel results
in this study.
Conceptually, openness to experience and intelligence appear to be most
dissimilar, whereas openness and intellectual brilliance seem most similar. For
example, individuals high on openness are imaginative, creative, cultured, inventive, unconventional, complex, reflective, curious, and have wide interests
(Rubenzer et al. 1996, p. 19), whereas those high on intelligence are smart,
bright, intelligent, have exceptional vocabularies, can leam many things quickly,
can do several things at once efficiently, and can see beyond facts to implications
(Rubenzer et al. 1996, p. 19). Individuals high on intellectual brilliance are
inventive, artistic, curious, intelligent, insightful, wise, sophisticated, idealistic,
complicated, have wide interests, and are neither dull nor commonplace (Simonton, 1986, p. 159). Even though the three "intelligence" variables are not synonymous, all three were correlated with the health behaviors. However, of the

Previous research on the association of openness and intelligence to health
behaviors is sparse, equivocal, often tangential, and pertains mostly to smoking.
For example, Coan, (1973) found smokers higher on openness to experience;
Booth-Kewley and Vickers (1994) found higher openness to experience was
related to substance abuse risk-taking in the form of smoking and drinking;
Matarazzo and Saslow (1960) and Walker, Nicholay, Kluczny, and Riedel (1969)
found that intelligence was not related to smoking; Ray (1985) found much less
smoking in an Australian MENSA sample than in the general population; and
Kubicka et al., (2001) found that smoking in adulthood was predicted by low IQ
in childhood.
Incidentally, some readers may be somewhat surprised that birth year, which
was used in this study as a time control variable, was correlated only with exercise. Some might have expected that birth year would have been positively correlated with the longevity of the presidents. This expectation occurs because of
an incorrect interpretation of the meaning of increases in life expectancy at birth
for the general public over the past 200 years. Increased life expectancy at birth
in our society has resulted mostly from a sharp decline in the incidence of infant
and child mortality, however, and the remaining life expectancy for those who
have survived well into adulthood has remained relatively stable over the course
of American history (e.g., Simonton, 1994, p. 212).
What I found in the present study was corroborating evidence for the importance of conscientiousness to a longer life and for suggested links between openness to experience and intelligence and tendencies to smoke, drink, and exercise.
Up until now, only Friedman and bis associates (1993, 1995) have offered evidence for the conscientiousness-longevity connection and the extant work on
openness and intelligence is limited and somewhat contradictory. Much more
research clearly is necessary to (a) further substantiate tbe link between conscientiousness and death age; (b) clarify the mechanisms by which conscientiousness may lead to a longer life; (c) substantiate tbe possible links between openness to experience and intelligence and smoking, drinking, and exercise, which
already have been rather firmly established as risk factors for early death; and (d)
determine the operative factors that may be involved in the association between
openness to experience and intelligence and smoking, drinking, and exercise.
