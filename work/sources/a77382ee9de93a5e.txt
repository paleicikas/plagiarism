ABSTRACT 
This study aimed to examine the relationships among loneliness, self-esteem, conformity, 
and online purchasing and gambling behaviors in Generation Z college students. To do 
so, a cross-sectional design was implemented with a sample of 69 undergraduate and 
graduate students who completed self-report measures assessing self-esteem, loneliness, 
conformity, psychological distress, online shopping addiction, and online gambling 
severity. Data were analyzed using SPSS through multiple and simple linear regression 
analyses, moderated regression, and Pearson correlations. Results indicated that selfesteem and loneliness were not significant predictors of conformity, and conformity did 
not predict online shopping or gambling behaviors. Additionally, self-esteem, loneliness, 
and conformity did not significantly predict online shopping addiction. However, lower 
self-esteem significantly predicted greater online gambling severity. Psychological 
distress partially moderated the relationship between loneliness and online shopping 
addiction but not gambling behavior. Screen time was also positively associated with 
both online shopping addiction and online gambling severity. Overall, these findings 
suggest that conformity may not be a central factor driving maladaptive online behaviors 
in Generation Z. Instead, psychological factors may play a stronger role in online 
gambling, while online shopping appears to be more influenced by environmental factors.

CHAPTER I 
LITERATURE REVIEW 
Introduction 
 
Loneliness is a profoundly human experience, rooted in our evolutionary drive for 
social connection (Cacioppo et al., 2014). Ironically, even in an age of constant digital 
communication, feelings of isolation are rising. The U.S. Surgeon General has labeled 
loneliness a public health crisis, particularly among young adults aged 18 to 34 (Connors, 
2024). Though social media platforms promise connection, many users report that online 
relationships often feel shallow or performative (Haimson et al., 2021). 
Loneliness impacts not only the mental health of those suffering from it but also 
their physical health. Several studies have shown that social isolation and loneliness 
affect health and mortality risk factors, such as high blood pressure, obesity, and 
smoking, as a coping mechanism (Singer, 2018). Social isolation can also contribute to 
higher cardiovascular disease risk by triggering greater sympathetic nervous system 
activity, elevating inflammation levels, and disrupting sleep, factors that can accelerate 
both brain and cardiovascular aging (Singer, 2018). 
The disconnect from social isolation can push people to seek comfort in other 
areas, such as shopping or gambling. Research has shown that loneliness can lead to 
emotionally driven consumption, often as a maladaptive coping mechanism (Kim, 2017). 
Influencers offer users a sense of connection with their curated content and relatable 
personas. This influence can deepen emotional dependence and reinforce materialistic 

 
2 
values, as individuals experiencing loneliness may find comfort in watching their favorite 
influencers and purchasing the products they promote as a way to cope. Influencer 
marketing and the addictive design of social media platforms both seem to contribute to 
this cycle. Platforms like Instagram and TikTok are designed to maximize user 
engagement through personalized content, which can lead to increased screen time, 
decreased self-esteem, and stronger feelings of isolation (Jouhki et al., 2022). These 
algorithms also amplify conformity by repeatedly exposing users to trending content and 
influencer-endorsed products (Babu et al., 2025; Golino, 2021). 
The Addictive Nature of Social Media and Influencer Marketing 
 
The presence of social media platforms has led to the emergence of behaviors like 
those of addiction, with research showing the compelling nature of instant gratification 
associated with digital engagement (Kumar & Mondal, 2018). The easy access to content, 
coupled with algorithm-driven content recommendations, can foster compulsive scrolling 
behavior, commonly referred to as "doom-scrolling" (Dodds, 2024). Short-form content, 
such as TikTok videos and Instagram Reels, reinforces this cycle by minimizing 
cognitive load and continuously presenting novel stimuli, reducing opportunities for 
reflective thought (Jouhki et al., 2022). 
"Doom-scrolling" has several negative impacts on users' mental health, including 
heightening depression, anxiety, and stress (Sekhon, 2024). This habit has also been 
strongly linked to poor sleep quality, social interaction anxiety, and emotional exhaustion 
(Anchan, 2025). These results are associated with many factors, one of which is a result 
of the disruption of sleep latency and efficiency from the blue light emitted by modern 
cell phone screens (Ayaki et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2018). Satici and colleagues also 

 
3 
found that doomscrolling was found to be significantly negatively associated with 
conscientiousness, extraversion, and agreeableness, while showing a significant positive 
association with neuroticism, consistent with prior research (Satici et al., 2023; Sharma et 
al., 2022). Additionally, doomscrolling was found to have positive relationships with 
FOMO and social media addiction (Satici et al., 2023). This suggests that individuals 
who engage in the habit are at greater risk of developing addictive behaviors and 
experiencing heightened feelings of loneliness. 
A key mechanism through which social media intensifies loneliness is influencer 
marketing. This strategy uses parasocial relationships, in which consumers develop onesided emotional bonds with influencers, leading to heightened trust in their 
recommendations (Karamustafic et al., 2020). Influencers effectively drive consumption 
patterns by creating an illusion of intimacy while reinforcing social comparison and selfesteem concerns. Studies indicate that influencer endorsements significantly enhance 
brand engagement and purchasing behaviors, with nano-influencers (1,000 followers) 
earning approximately $315 per post. Conversely, macro-influencers (more than 1 
million followers) generate between $100,000 and $250,000 (Conklin, 2020). These 
dynamics contribute to a self-perpetuating cycle wherein individuals seek social 
validation through consumption, further deepening their feelings of isolation (Liu & Lee, 
2024). 
A scoping review of studies on personalized user experiences on social media 
conducted by Ragnild and colleagues (2023) found a total of 13 articles, including 
evidence that targeted advertisements raise notable privacy concerns, especially for 
children and adolescents, who often lack awareness of how algorithms work and are more 

 
4 
easily influenced by influencer marketing (Ragnild et al., 2023). They also found that 
overall awareness of how algorithms work to curate your feed was generally low, and 
that awareness could help users better understand the logic behind said algorithms, 
leading to less susceptibility (Ragnild et al., 2023). 
Escapism and Algorithmic Reinforcement 
 
Algorithms are designed to sustain attention, but feelings needing to escape the 
real world could be the real reason for the initial engagement with social media. 
Escapism, defined as the tendency to avoid real-world stressors through digital 
distraction, plays a significant role in perpetuating social media engagement (Jouhki et 
al., 2022). The design of social media platforms relies on personalized algorithms that 
match content to user engagement, thereby encouraging sustained engagement (Ragnhild, 
2023). These algorithms curate content based on user behavior, creating "echo chambers" 
that reinforce pre-existing beliefs and consumption patterns (Golino, 2021). There are 
several dangers of "echo chambers," including isolating users from the diversity of 
content available; they become stuck in their ideas and reinforce pre-conceptions, 
ultimately influencing the user's self and world views (Head et al., 2020; Otto & Maier, 
2016; Ragnhild, 2023). These "echo chambers" also have been shown to intensify 
materialistic tendencies and contribute to compulsive spending behaviors (Tian et al., 
2022). 
Research also shows that algorithmic reinforcement tends to boost conformity by 
repeatedly showing users popular content, which pulls them further into a digital world 
focused on consumerism (Babu et al., 2025). Targeted advertising, powered by datadriven insights, enables brands to align their marketing strategies with Gen Z's digital 

 
5 
behaviors, resulting in heightened susceptibility and vulnerability to influencer 
endorsements (Zhang & Wei, 2021). As a result, individuals become deeply rooted in 
cycles of consumption that provide only temporary relief from their underlying loneliness 
and self-esteem issues. 
This algorithm, while continuously feeding the user videos they enjoy, also 
increases their chances of addiction (Liu et al., 2021). With short-form content, users are 
given short-term rewards and instant gratification. In other words, when they do not 
enjoy a video, all a user needs to do is scroll to one that causes enjoyment, and once 
interacted with, the algorithm updates and queues up similar content to continue and 
encourage further user engagement (Brand et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2021). When people 
feel their self-esteem is hurt or lonely, they tend to find a way to "cope" with said 
feelings; this idea is called "self-compensation motivation" (Chu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 
2021). This motivation can encourage users to use short-form content to escape their 
feelings by doom-scrolling content until they forget the stress of real life (Young et al., 
2017). This can lead to the overuse of technology, which is like an addiction, as the user 
develops a habit of using their phone to escape reality. 
Overuse or maladaptive use of social media can often develop into social media 
addiction. Social media addiction, like gambling addiction, is an impulse control 
disorder, meaning that they are often developed due to a lack of impulse control 
(Benavides et al., 2023). Developing social media addiction can be linked to having sleep 
disorders, anxiety or depression, familial discomfort or discourse, and academic situation 
stressors (Benavides et al., 2023). It was also found that the consequences of social media 
addiction can lead to depression, anxiety, insomnia, decreased life satisfaction, and affect 

 
6 
academic performance (Benavides et al., 2023). Algorithms designed to keep users' 
attention could ultimately positively influence the development of a social media 
addiction. With the disorder being an impulse control disorder, it is likely that a user with 
impulse control issues, given access to unlimited content, will experience problems. At 
the same time, scrolling would not be able to self-monitor their own usage of social 
media. 
Algorithms can target demographics differently, especially when it comes to 
genders and those with health concerns, as found by a study conducted by Bol and 
colleagues (Bol et al., 2020). In their research, it was found that Facebook, used as a case 
study, used a complex algorithm that fed content based on the age, gender, 
socioeconomic class, and race of the users. Age was significantly related to brand content 
exposure; as younger users were more likely to be shown beauty and fashion content (OR 
= .95, 95% CI) while older users were more likely to be targeted with health-related 
advertisements (OR = 1.08, 95% CI) or political content (OR = 1.34, 95% CI; Bol et al., 
2018). Gender differences were also evident as men were targeted with advertisements 
about cars (OR = 9.55, 95% CI) and electronics (OR = 7.73, 95% CI). In contrast, women 
were targeted with advertisements on health-related content (OR = 0.05, 95% CI; Bol et 
al., 2018). 
Psychological Implications: Low Self-Esteem and Social Isolation 
The relationship between social media use and self-perception has been widely 
studied, with findings indicating a negative correlation between prolonged digital 
engagement and overall well-being (Huang, 2017). Social comparison theory proposes 
that individuals evaluate themselves based on perceived standards set by their peers, a 

 
7 
process exacerbated by curated online personas and profiles (Holland & Tiggemann, 
2016). As users are continuously exposed to idealized representations of beauty, success, 
and lifestyle, dissatisfaction with one's circumstances may increase, leading to 
diminished self-esteem and heightened loneliness. Furthermore, research shows the 
paradox of digital connectivity: while social media enables frequent interactions, these 
exchanges often lack depth and emotional fulfillment, ultimately reinforcing social 
isolation (Connors, 2024). Users who engage in excessive online interactions may 
experience reduced motivation for in-person socialization, further contributing to the 
loneliness epidemic. 
 
A study by Pertiwi and colleagues confirmed that Gen Z's dependence on social 
media mediated the relationship between self-esteem and psychological well-being, 
which suggests that while self-esteem does play a more direct role in influencing 
psychological well-being, the dependency on social media can still significantly influence 
that connection (Pertiwi et al., 2022). They also found that motives for using social media 
also affect the relationship, and that those who use social media to keep in touch with 
their older friends are more heavily influenced by the negative impact of social media 
dependency on their psychological well-being than those who use social media to meet 
new people (Pertiwi et al., 2022). However, those who use social media to meet new 
people are more susceptible to potential risks of catfishing and vulnerability to harmful 
online interactions (Pertiwi et al., 2022). 
 
A review of 42 studies that examined the connection between social media and 
the increased rate of depression in adolescents found that a majority of the studies found 
a positive correlation between the increased rates of depression and the time spent on 

 
8 
social media (Vidal et al., 2022). It also found that in longitudinal studies such as Frison 
and Eggermont (2015), the frequency of using Instagram could predict depressed mood 
in users six months later (Frison & Eggermont, 2015; Vidal et al., 2022). Woods and 
Scott (2016) found that the usage of social media at night was associated with anxiety 
symptoms and poor sleep quality (Vidal et al., 2022; Woods & Scott, 2016). It was also 
found that there was a positive correlation between higher rates of social media usage and 
suicidal ideation and attempts (Sampasa-Kanyinga & Hamilton, 2015; Sampasa-
Kanyinga & Lewis, 2015; Vidal et al., 2022). 
Gen Z's Need to Consume to Cope 
During the COVID-19 pandemic, Gen Z found themselves stuck inside and 
missing out on many pivotal moments of their lives, including day-to-day high school 
experiences with peers, prom, graduation, and college experiences. According to Parker 
(2020), the reason for the increased rates of depression in Gen Z is due to a lack of faceto-face social interactions. So if Gen Z is depressed, how might they cope? The answer 
could be retail therapy. 
 
Bisht (2024) calls retail therapy a consumption of goods compensating for 
psychosocial dysfunctions such as low self-esteem and loss of control, which assists in 
relieving the shoppers' mood (Bisht & Desu, 2024). Retail therapy is a short-term reward 
behavior that can act to provide relief, though not relief that will last; it also can lead to 
maladaptive behaviors such as a shopping addiction (Bisht & Desu, 2024). 
During COVID-19, data from 889 US consumers were studied. The findings 
showed that the fear of lockdown, limited access to resources, peers buying, and panic 
buying had a positive influence on impulsive purchasing behavior (Ahmed et al., 2020). 

 
9 
The findings also show that fear appeal and fake news propagated online via social media 
positively impacted impulsive buying behavior (Ahmed et al., 2020). 
 
Purwaningdyah and Pratminingsih (2025) created a study to explore how social 
media-induced stress influences online shopping habits in Gen Z. They found that stress 
from social media has not only indirect effects on online shopping habits but also direct 
effects, which demonstrates that negative emotional response, such as stress that is 
caused by social media, can lead individuals in Gen Z to seek out immediate gratification 
and relief through negative coping skills such as shopping (Purwaningdyah & 
Pratminigsih, 2025). 
 
A recent study conducted in China found that the stress of college, such as 
academic stress, stress from transitioning to college life, financial stress, and 
interpersonal relationships, triggered online shopping addiction tendencies (Li et al., 
2023). Li and colleagues found that students who reported higher levels of stress also 
reported a higher prevalence of online shopping addiction tendencies and lower social 
support. Social support provided to college students is a protective factor against online 
shopping addiction, so the lack of it makes them more vulnerable to developing an 
addiction, proving that loneliness could also lead to an increase in purchasing behavior in 
college-aged students (Li et al., 2023). The study also found no significant differences 
between genders, meaning that male and female students were affected the same. 
Consumerism in the Digital Age 
It has been predicted that consumerism will be heavily affected by marketing in 
the digital age through targeted advertising on applications such as Instagram, Facebook, 
and Twitter (Lucius & Hanson, 2016). Nowadays, the applications most used, according 

 
10 
to a poll published by Pew Research, are YouTube, Facebook, Instagram, Pinterest, and 
TikTok (Sidoti et al., 2024). Four of these applications, Instagram, Pinterest, Facebook, 
and TikTok, were initially set up with "scrolling," allowing the user to view content 
continuously. Now, YouTube has joined in with their new feature "YouTube Shorts," 
which focuses on the use of short-form content of short videos that can be viewed and 
scrolled through similarly to TikTok's content and Instagram Reels. 
 
The structure of these platforms is not incidental; it has direct implications for 
consumerism. Kucuk (2016) stresses that consumerism in the digital age is characterized 
by blurring boundaries between personal choice and corporate influence. The constant 
stream of content enables marketers to embed advertisements seamlessly into users' 
everyday digital experiences, encouraging impulse buying and shaping consumer identity 
in subtle but powerful ways. Similarly, Durai and King (2019) found that the growth of 
consumerism is closely tied to digital marketing strategies, which rely on user data to 
target individuals with tailored promotions that maximize engagement and purchasing 
behavior. 
 
These marketing strategies particularly influence younger populations, especially 
college students. A study conducted by Bizel and colleagues found that adult TikTok, one 
of the most prominent social media applications used by college students, users ages 25-
40 are not as heavily influenced by TikTok as their younger counterparts, i.e., the 
average-aged college students (Bizel et al., 2022). Maulidy, Zaini, and Sanjani (2025) 
found that social media is critical in shaping consumption trends such as online gambling, 
mobile games, and purchases on applications like Shopee, among students, by reinforcing 
conformity to popular products and lifestyles. Peer influence and the visibility of 

 
11 
consumer choices online encourage individuals to participate in collective trends rather 
than make entirely independent purchasing decisions. This shows how consumerism in 
the digital age is not only economic but also social, which needs to be further researched 
to better understand consumer trends and how they connect to the mental health of 
college students. 
 
These patterns can also have deeper psychological and cultural impacts. Clark 
(2024) writes that consumerism in the digital age affects spending habits and contributes 
to cultural adaptation processes, as individuals often adopt consumption patterns of the 
dominant online culture. Therefore, consumerism in the digital age is both a social and 
psychological phenomenon, driven by targeted marketing, peer influence, and the 
technological structures of platforms themselves. 
 
A new platform that cashes in on these patterns is TikTok Shop. TikTok Shop 
defines itself as: 
an in-app e-commerce solution that seamlessly blends entertainment and shopping 
by allowing brands and creators to sell products directly on the platform. It turns 
casual browsing into a comprehensive shopping journey, where users can 
discover and purchase items without ever leaving the app. (TikTok for Business, 
2025) 
There are not many studies done on the effectiveness of TikTok Shop, but one study 
conducted by Putri and colleagues investigated how Gen Z's TikTok Shop users' trust in 
sellers and privacy influence their purchasing decisions on the application (Putri et al., 
2023). Their results found that if a purchase had some type of security seal, the user was 
more likely to purchase from that seller and, if the user trusted the seller, as in if it was an 

 
12 
influencer they were familiar with, they were more likely to purchase as well (Putri et al., 
2023). 
 
A new concern for this new digital age of consumerism is online gambling and 
advertisements utilized by social media platforms that could target vulnerable 
demographics. As discussed previously, algorithmic targeting does exist, and many 
platforms that use it have access to user data such as socioeconomic status, age, gender, 
and race. A study conducted by Lind and colleagues in 2022 found that the most 
susceptible demographic to gambling, specifically online gambling, was males aged from 
18 to 54 with a higher income (Lind et al., 2022). All details of this and many other 
demographics are known by social media algorithms and used for advertising. 
Digital Gambling Addiction and Gen Z 
Gambling has drastically changed in recent years, as now, rather than going to a 
casino, many turn to their phones instead (Ukhov et al., 2021). The accessibility of this 
new form of gambling has undoubtedly caused an increase in users, which is cause for 
concern as gambling, in any form, is ultimately addictive (Gainsbury, 2015). Gen Z is 
one of the more actively online generations, so they are more likely to be exposed to 
dangerous online habits (Alobidyeen, 2023). A particularly vulnerable group of Gen Z to 
online gambling is college students (Debora & Wijaya, 2025). A systematic review on 
online gambling marketing found that the primary emerging trend of online gambling is 
sports betting (Guillou-Landreat et al., 2021). This trend is problematic for college-age 
students due to college sports. A study conducted by O'Brate at Kansas City University 
surveyed 115 students to explore how and why college students watch football. The 
results showed that over half of the participants (N = 58, 50%) preferred attending in-

 
13 
person games (O'Brate, 2021). Among alternative viewing methods, streaming emerged 
as the most popular option, followed by social media (O'Brate, 2021). 
 
Online gambling has several adverse effects on college students, such as social 
isolation, an increased risk of criminal behavior, and severe financial loss (Debora & 
Wijaya, 2025; Mahilda & Setiawan, 2025; Nayottama, 2024). When online gambling can 
lead to social isolation, that often leads to using technology more to cope with said 
isolation, leading to a vicious cycle. 
 
Contributing to this cycle are advertisements that have become more prevalent 
online. In a survey conducted by St. Bonaventure and Siena College, it was found that 
nearly 60% of Americans have seen online gambling advertisements, and that 22% of 
Americans, including 48% of men 18 to 49 years old, have an account with at least one 
online sportsbook (Siena College, 2025). Men online, especially those in Gen Z, are 
typically targeted by advertisements about gambling, including those that utilize social 
media marketing, such as influencer marketing (Guillou-Landreat et al., 2021). It is 
primarily men who are users of online gambling apps, as it is men who are about 4.8 
times more likely, as it was reported that 24% of men had an online gambling profile 
while only 4% of women had one (St. Bonaventure, 2025). 
 
Another study conducted by Rossi and Nairn sampled from 650 participants aged 
11-78 and found that just about half (45.2%) of 11-17-year-olds and almost threequarters (72.4%) of 18-24-year-olds reported seeing gambling advertisements on their 
social media feeds at least once a week (Rossi & Nairn, 2021). Daily exposure was also 
found to occur every day, with one in four children (11-17) and adults (24-78), and as 
many as one in three young adults (18-24), stating they encounter such adverts every day 

 
14 
(Rossi & Nairn, 2021). They also found that gambling content, such as influencers who 
make content primarily focused on gambling, was more appealing to children and young 
persons (0.9 and 0.98) than to adults (-1.75), the difference between the two being 
statistically significant (Rossi & Nairn, 2021). 
 
Another study by Rossi and colleagues aimed to investigate the volume, content, 
audience engagement, and regulatory compliance of online gambling advertisements in 
the United Kingdom (U.K.; Rossi et al., 2021). The study found that gambling 
advertising on Twitter was widespread, as they analyzed 890,000 ads (Rossini et al., 
2021). They found that regulatory compliance is poor, as two-thirds of the advertisements 
failed to meet U.K. standards (Rossi et al., 2021). They also discovered that 41,000 
children in the U.K. follow gambling accounts, which is especially concerning when 
considering the last finding, which was that e-sport gambling advertisements were 
drawing disproportionate attention (28%) from users aged under 16 (Rossi et al., 2021). 
Social Influence Theory and Conformity Online 
Social influence theory examines how individual cognition and behavior are 
shaped by interpersonal and group dynamics. Herber Kelman originally proposed the 
theory in 1953 and focuses on three aspects: compliance, identification, and 
internalization. Kelman explains that these processes are influenced by either the desire 
to be socially accepted by others, known as normative influence, or by the belief that 
others have more accurate information, which is known as informational influence 
(Kelman, 1974). 
 
Social influence theory shows that influence is not always the same. Sometimes, it 
can be temporary, i.e., due to compliance; sometimes it is tied to belonging, i.e., 

 
15 
identification; or sometimes it can be permanent, i.e., internalization. With social media, 
influencers or advertising usually target identification and internalization to sell products 
to users as they shape fashion, opinions, and trends (Davlembayeva & Papagiannidis, 
2025). 
 
Social media has the possibility to amplify the ideas behind social influence 
theory. It is shown through normative influence, as users are reinforced through visible 
markers of approval, such as likes, shares, followers, and views. Over time, these 
reinforcers create pressure to conform to online trends to gain more social validation. 
Informational influence is also heightened online as users turn to influencers, peers, and 
trending content to learn what is currently "on trend." These dynamics influence users' 
values, self-concept, and consumer habits (Davlembayeva & Papagiannidis, 2025). 
Gaps in the Literature and Future Research Directions 
Despite extensive research on the immediate psychological effects of social 
media, significant gaps remain regarding the long-term implications of digital 
engagement on emotional well-being. Specifically, studies have yet to examine the 
sustained impact of algorithm-driven conformity on consumer behavior and social 
isolation. Additionally, much of the existing literature is Western-centric, overlooking 
cultural implications in social media influence and digital consumption patterns (Tian et 
al., 2022). 
 
More gaps in the literature that call for further investigation are the effects and 
prevalence of online gambling behaviors and habits in college students, primarily those in 
Gen Z (Rossi & Nairn, 2021). Most of the studies available now are not based in the 
United States, as online gambling has changed so rapidly in the United States (Guillou 

 
16 
Landreat et al., 2021). There is also a lack of studies investigating the effect of influencer 
marketing of online gambling in the United States and how that might influence Gen Z's 
use of online gambling. Lastly, the relationship between the rates of self-esteem and 
loneliness in Gen Z college students and the prevalence of online gambling behaviors 
advertised by influencers on social media has not yet been investigated. 
 
The rapid evolution of platforms such as TikTok, Instagram Reels, and AI-driven 
digital communities justifies further investigation into their role in shaping current social 
norms and interpersonal relationships. By addressing these gaps, researchers can provide 
a more comprehensive framework for understanding the relationship between digital 
engagement and loneliness among Gen Z.

CHAPTER II 
METHODOLOGY 
Participants 
 
The primary recruitment sample for this study was undergraduate and graduate 
students at Abilene Christian University, a private, faith-based university in Abilene, 
Texas. Recruitment for this study primarily consisted of requests to professors to 
distribute an electronic link to their students that provides study information, access to the 
informed consent page, and, finally, access to the study survey. When possible, the 
primary investigator provided professors with a QR code that students could scan to 
access the study information and informed consent page. 
Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria 
 
For a participant to be included in the study, they must have been between the 
ages of 18 and 25 and users of at least one social media platform (e.g., TikTok, 
Instagram, YouTube, etc.). Individuals outside the 18 to 25 age range or not using at least 
one social media platform were excluded from the study. Participation was voluntary, 
informed consent was obtained following Institutional Review Board (IRB) guidelines 
and occurred before completion of any survey items. Demographic information collected 
included age, gender, major, ethnicity, frequency of social media use, and which social 
media platforms the participant uses the most/prefers. 
 
 

 
18 
Design and Procedure 
This study employed a cross-sectional, correlational design using a self-report 
online survey. Participants first provided informed consent and acknowledged that they 
were at least 18 years of age. After providing informed consent, participants were 
directed to a single survey where participants completed a single survey made up of six 
separate scales: the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (Rosenberg, 1965), the Mehrabian 
Conformity Scale (Zlobina, 2023), the Online Shopping Addiction Scale (Zhao et al., 
2017), the UCLA Loneliness scale (Russell, 2010), the Online Gambling Disorder 
Questionnaire (OGD-Q; González-Cabrera et al., 2020), and the Depression Anxiety 
Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21; Moya et al., 2022). The survey was administered via a secure 
online platform, Google Forms, and was designed to take approximately 10-15 minutes 
to complete. The datum was securely stored and subsequently analyzed using SPSS and 
Jamovi to examine the relationships between study variables (e.g., social media usage, 
self-esteem, conformity tendencies, loneliness, consumer behavior, online gambling 
disorder, and symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress). 
Measures 
The Conformity Scale 
The Conformity Scale (Mehrabian & Stefl, 1995) is an 11-item scale assessing 
conformity. Individuals are asked to answer questions related to conformity, such as "I 
often rely on, and act on, the advice of others" (Mehrabian & Stefl, 1995; Zlobina, 2023). 
A full list of questions is provided in Appendix B. Responses are given on a 7-point 
Likert scale from 1 (Not at all true of me) to 7 (Extremely true of me; Mehrabian & Stefl, 
1995). The Mehrabian Conformity Scale has demonstrated convergent and discriminant 

 
19 
validity and satisfactory internal consistency, with an original Cronbach's alpha score of 
0.77 (Mehrabian & Stefl, 1995; Zlobina, 2023). 
Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES) 
The Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (Rosenberg, 1979) is a 10-item scale assessing 
self-esteem. Individuals are asked questions related to self-esteem, such as "On the 
whole, I am satisfied with myself." A full list of questions on the scale is in Appendix B. 
Responses are given on a 4-point Likert scale 1 being "strongly agree" and 4 being 
"strongly disagree" (Avison, 1981). The scale has demonstrated internal consistency, 
test-retest reliability, and concurrent, predictive, and construct validity, with a Cronbach's 
alpha in the range of .77 to .88 (Rosenberg, 1979). 
The Online Shopping Addiction Scale 
The Online Shopping Addiction Scale is an 18-item scale developed to assess 
addictive online shopping behavior across six domains: salience, mood modification, 
tolerance, withdrawal, conflict, and relapse. Individuals are asked to respond to items 
such as, "When I am not shopping online, I keep thinking about it." A full list of 
questions is in Appendix B. Responses are given on a 5-point Likert scale, with 1 being 
"strongly disagree" and five being "strongly agree." The scale demonstrated high 
reliability, with Cronbach's alpha values of 0.90 to 0.95. (Zhao, 2017). 
UCLA Loneliness Scale Version 3 
The UCLA Loneliness Scale Version 3 (Russell, 2010) is a 20-item scale 
assessing how often a person feels disconnected from others (Russell, 2010). Individuals 
are asked to answer questions about feelings of loneliness, such as "How often do you 
feel alone?" A full list of questions is in Appendix B. Responses are given on a 4-point 

 
20 
rating scale anchored by 1 being "never" and 4 being "always." The scale measure is 
highly reliable regarding internal consistency and test-retest reliability with a Cronbach's 
alpha ranging from 0.89 to 0.94 (Russell, 2010). 
The Online Gambling Disorder Questionnaire (OGD-Q) 
The Online Gambling Disorder Questionnaire (González-Cabrera et al., 2020). is 
an 11-item scale developed to assess symptoms of online gambling disorder in 
adolescents (González-Cabrera et al., 2020). Individuals are asked to respond to questions 
such as, "Do you feel nervous, irritated, or angry when trying to reduce or stop gambling 
online?" Responses are provided on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 "never" to 5 
"every day," with higher scores reflecting greater severity of gambling-related problems. 
The OGD-Q demonstrated strong validity, supported by content, construct, and 
convergent analyses, and high internal consistency reliability with a Cronbach's alpha of 
0.94 (α = 0.94; ω = 0.95; González-Cabrera et al., 2020). 
The Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) 
The Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) is a 21-item scale developed 
to assess symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress (Moya et al., 2022). The scale 
contains three subscales, each with seven items. Individuals are asked to respond to items 
such as, "I found it difficult to work up the initiative to do things." Responses are given 
on a 4-point Likert scale anchored with 0 being "did not apply to me at all" and 3 being 
"applied to me very much, or most of the time." The DASS-21 demonstrated acceptable 
validity and reliability, with overall Cronbach's alpha of .74 and ordinal alpha 
coefficients indicating good reliability across subscales (depression = .83, anxiety = .74, 
stress = .87). Criterion validity was supported by the depression subscale's ability to 

 
21 
discriminate cases of depression compared with the Structured Clinical Interview for 
DSM-IV (AUC = .76), and convergent validity was demonstrated through a moderate 
correlation with the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (r = .61, p < .001; Moya et al., 
2022). 
Hypotheses 
Hypothesis 1 
It is hypothesized that lower self-esteem and higher loneliness will be associated 
with higher levels of conformity among Generation Z college students. 
Hypothesis 2 
It is hypothesized that higher levels of conformity will be associated with greater 
susceptibility to conformity-driven online purchasing behaviors. 
Hypothesis 3 
It is hypothesized that lower self-esteem, higher loneliness, and higher conformity 
will predict greater susceptibility to conformity-driven online purchasing behaviors 
among Generation Z college students. 
Hypothesis 4 
It is hypothesized that lower self-esteem, higher loneliness, and higher conformity 
will predict greater severity of online gambling-related problems among Generation Z 
college students. 
Hypothesis 5 
It is hypothesized that psychological distress will moderate the relationships 
between psychological vulnerability (self-esteem and loneliness) and conformity-driven 
behavioral outcomes. 

 
22 
Hypothesis 6 
It is hypothesized that frequency of social media or screen use will be positively 
associated with conformity, online shopping addiction, and online gambling severity.

RESULTS 
Participants 
 
Participants were sixty-nine (69) undergraduate and graduate students at Abilene 
Christian University. Participants in this sample had an average age of 20.1 years old and 
were primarily female (75.4%, n = 52) and White/Caucasian (68.1%, n = 47), followed 
by Hispanic/Latino (15.9%, n = 11), and Black/African American (14.5%, n = 10). The 
most frequently reported social media platform used was Instagram (94.2%), followed by 
YouTube (65.2%), and TikTok (53.6%). The most frequent amount of daily screen time 
used for leisure purposes was 5-6 hours (33.3%, n = 23), followed by 3-4 hours (29%, n 
= 20), and 7-8 hours (17.4%, n = 12). 
Descriptive Statistics 
 
Descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables, including screen time, 
online shopping addiction, loneliness, online gambling disorder, psychological distress, 
depression, anxiety, stress, conformity, and self-esteem. Participants reported an average 
of 4.93 hours of daily screen time (SD = 2.28). Mean scores for behavioral variables 
included online shopping addiction (M = 33.40, SD = 13.60) and online gambling 
disorder (M = 12.60, SD = 3.89). For mental health outcomes, the psychological distress

24 
= 13.30, SD = 8.73). Conformity had a mean of -1.08 (SD = 9.76), loneliness (M = 44.90, 
SD = 11.30), and self-esteem averaged 19.10 (SD = 5.26). 
Most variables demonstrated acceptable levels of skewness and kurtosis, 
suggesting approximate normality. Screen time (skew = 0.27), loneliness (skew = 0.31), 
and self-esteem (skew = -0.21) were approximately symmetric. Online Shopping 
Addiction (skew = 0.91), psychological distress (skew = 0.99), stress (skew = 0.61), and

One variable, Online Gambling Disorder, displayed substantial non-normality,

strongly right-skewed distribution with heavy tails. This pattern suggests that most 
participants reported relatively low levels of online gambling symptoms, with a small 
number of participants reporting substantially higher scores.

CHAPTER IV 
DISCUSSION 
Summarized Findings 
 
Contrary to expectations, it was found that self-esteem and loneliness were not 
significant predictors of conformity in this sample. Conformity did not predict either 
online shopping behavior or online gambling behavior. These findings challenged the 
assumption that conformity might serve as the primary mechanism linking internal 
psychological vulnerability to externally influenced digital behaviors. Instead, the results 
suggest that conformity may not be a central pathway through which Generation Z 
individuals engage in maladaptive online consumption. 
 
It was also found that neither lower self-esteem, higher rates of loneliness, nor 
higher rates of conformity predicted greater susceptibility to conformity-driven online 
purchasing habits. There was partial support that lower self-esteem, higher rates of 
loneliness, and higher conformity predict online gambling severity. Lastly, partial 
support was found for psychological distress moderating the relationship between 
psychological vulnerability (self-esteem and loneliness) and online shopping addiction 
behaviors, but not severe online gambling behaviors. 
Interpretation 
 
These findings suggest that conformity is not a predictor of online shopping 
behavior nor online gambling behavior. The literature, specifically social influence 
theory, suggests that social media could act as a conformity pressure by leveraging peer 

 
38 
norms, curated content, and algorithm-driven interactions that create a sense of social 
validation and urgency (Davlembayeva & Papagiannidis, 2025; Kelman, 1974). Despite 
the theory and prior research, the present study found no significant evidence supporting 
this. This could be due to the unique forms of conformity found online, which may not 
fit the survey chosen. 
 
The present study also found that self-esteem and loneliness were not predictors 
of conformity, contrary to prior findings in the literature (Cacioppo et al., 2014; Haimson 
et al., 2021; Kim, 2017; Pertiwi et al., 2022). This could also point to online conformity 
manifesting differently than direct conformity pressures in reality, as with influencer 
marketing, the fear of missing out (FOMO), and pressures to gain followers/likes, it is 
likely conformity is exhibited differently (Davlembayeva & Papagiannidis, 2025). 
 
The finding that lower self-esteem, higher loneliness, and greater conformity did 
not predict susceptibility to conformity-driven online purchasing, while showing partial 
support in predicting online gambling severity, may reflect important differences in the 
underlying psychological mechanisms of these behaviors. Online purchasing decisions, 
even while socially influenced, could be driven more by external factors (e.g., influencer 
marketing strategies, product appeal and financial considerations) rather than internal 
factors such as loneliness and self-esteem. With online gambling it may be that 
individuals with lower self-esteem or higher loneliness may be more likely to use 
gambling as a means of mood regulation, escape or validation (Alobidyeen, 2023; Debora 
& Wijaya, 2025; Gainsbury, 2016; Ukhov et al., 2021). 
Additionally, social influence in gambling may be more explicit and reinforcing 
through the normalization of online gambling, visible wins, and more interactive features 

 
39 
which work together to amplify the role of conformity (Rossi et al., 2021; Rossi & Nairn, 
2021; St. Bonaventure, 2025). Together both findings suggest that while both behaviors 
occur in more socially influential online spaces, the extent to which individualistic 
psychological mechanisms predict the enablement in said behaviors may depend on the 
degree to which each behavior fulfills those psychological needs. 
 
Lastly the partial support found for psychological distress as a moderator in the 
relationship between psychological vulnerability and online shopping addiction behavior 
but not severe online gambling behaviors could be due to the possibility that shopping as 
a behavior is more socially acceptable that gambling behaviors (Bizel et al., 2022; Lind et 
al., 2022; Putri et al., 2023). These findings suggest that psychological distress may 
differentially influence the expression of addictive behaviors by how readily the behavior 
serves as an accessible and socially reinforced coping mechanism. 
 
The sample itself had a psychological distress composite score averaged 32.80 
(SD = 25.00). Subscale means for psychological distress included depression (M = 9.76, 
SD = 9.76), anxiety (M = 9.85, SD = 9.02), and stress (M = 13.30, SD = 8.73). According 
to the scoring guide for the DASS 21 the present study's sample average was just outside 
the normal range (0-9) for depression with a mean of M = 9.76, likely indicating the 
sample is largely not experiencing clinical levels of depression. The sample did however 
score between the mild (8-9) and the moderate range of anxiety (10-14) with a mean of 
M=9.85, indicating the sample was mildly to moderately anxious. Lastly the sample's 
mean for stress fell within the normal range (0-14) with a mean of M = 13.30, indicating 
the sample was not largely experiencing large amounts of stress during the time of data 
collection. 

 
40 
Integration 
This study sought to better understand and investigate how psychological 
vulnerabilities and social influences interact to predict online purchasing and online 
gambling habits. Taken together the results suggest that self-esteem, loneliness and 
conformity operate differently in with the different online maladaptive coping strategies 
that are online gambling and online purchasing behaviors as there was more evidence of 
associations with online gambling than conformity-driven purchasing. There was also 
evidence that the moderating role of psychological distress seems to be more relevant for 
online shopping than online gambling, which could be relevant when used in treatment 
contexts for shopping addiction. Overall, this study has shown the unique distinction 
between the two maladaptive coping strategies when looking at risk factors such as selfesteem, loneliness and conformity as well as the need for more targeted and contextspecific approaches to prevention and intervention. 
Limitations 
 
Several limitations were present when interpreting the results. Firstly, the small 
sample size (n = 69) may have limited the statistical power of the results and reduced the 
ability to detect small but significant relationships between the variables. Post hoc 
sensitivity analyses indicated that, given the obtained sample sizes across analyses, the 
study was generally powered to detect medium-sized effects but was underpowered to 
detect small effects. Specifically, regression models were sufficiently powered (80%) to 
detect effects ranging from approximately f2 = .12 to f2 = .24, corresponding to R2 values 
of approximately .11 to .19 depending on the number of predictors included. Similarly, 

 
41 
for bivariate correlations, the study was powered to detect effects of approximately ∣r∣ = 
.33 to .35. 
Additionally, limitations include that the majority of the sample was women (n = 
52) to men (n = 17) and the cross-sectional design prevents conclusions about causal 
relationships between psychological vulnerability and problematic behaviors online. 
Additionally, due to the sample being from a private Christian college and distributed by 
university professors, many subjects may have felt uncomfortable endorsing problematic 
online behaviors such as shopping addiction or gambling habits, leading to lower levels 
of reported symptoms. Also, the distribution of online gambling severity scores was 
highly skewed, with most participants reporting minimal gambling involvement. This 
pattern is often common in nonclinical samples but could reduce variability, further 
limiting the detection of significant predictors. Lastly, the study relied on self-report 
measures, which may be subject to response bias. 
Conclusion 
 
The purpose of this study was to investigate the relationship between self-esteem, 
loneliness, and conformity pressures with online gambling and online consumerism 
habits in Generation Z college students. It was found that lower self-esteem, high 
loneliness and greater conformity did not significantly predict susceptibility to 
conformity-driven purchasing behavior. However, partial support was found for these 
variables in predicting online gambling severity which may suggest a stronger 
relationship between psychological vulnerabilities and engagement in said behavior. 
Lastly psychological distress emerged as a partial moderator in the relationship between 
the psychological vulnerabilities and online shopping behaviors. 

 
42 
 
This study has shown a need for the importance of distinguishing the difference 
between the psychological reasonings between engagement of these behaviors, as they 
appear to be driven by different internal mechanisms. While online gambling seems to 
be more closely tied to underlying vulnerabilities and potentially maladaptive coping 
behaviors, online shopping behaviors may be instead more influenced by situational and 
environmental factors such as marketing and social media exposure. These results could 
help contribute to a much more nuanced understanding of how social influence and 
psychological vulnerabilities interact with the digital space, 
Future Directions 
 
Future research should continue to explore these distinctions by examining more 
diverse and larger samples. The sample for this study was taken from Abilene Christian 
University which contributed to the smaller sample and also a sample that has many 
spiritual protective factors, so future research should focus on larger universities. By 
focusing on public, larger universities, future research could also focus on samples of 
college students with nationally known sporting conferences which could lead to more 
incentive and pressure to engage in online gambling habits. Additionally, research 
investing platform specific features such as social media algorithms, and influencer 
content may help clarify which mechanisms may affect conformity in online shopping 
and gambling contexts. Finally, future work should work on exploring intervention 
strategies that are tailored to account for these found differences.
