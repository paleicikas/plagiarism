Abstract 
Heavy alcohol consumption is associated with a range of psychosocial problems and 
chronic conditions, resulting in approximately 140,000 preventable deaths each year. With these 
potential consequences in mind, it is understandable that 46% of individuals who consume 
alcohol report attempting to reduce the quantity of alcohol they drink; however, very few enter 
formal treatment. Despite this, many people will decrease their heavy drinking over time, 
presumably due to individual and environmental factors, particularly self-efficacy and protective 
social determinants of health (SDoH). This study aimed to answer the research question "How is 
SDoH adversity related to the likelihood of reporting reduced alcohol use and to what extent 
does self-efficacy moderate this relationship?" Results of a moderated logistic regression model 
were statistically significant; however, the interaction term was nonsignificant, indicating that 
self-efficacy did not moderate the relationship between SDoH adversity and the likelihood of 
participants reporting reduce alcohol use. A follow-up logistic regression, testing main effects 
only, indicated a significant main effect for SDoH adversity, indicating that as SDoH adversity 
increased, the odds of reporting reduced alcohol consumption decreased. In other words, 
individuals who experience greater SDoH adversity were less likely to report reduced alcohol 
use. There was no significant main effect for self-efficacy, suggesting self-efficacy does not 
significantly impact the odds of reporting reduced alcohol consumption. Future research should 
continue to explore differences in self-efficacy and experiences of SDoH adversity across the 
spectrum of recovery and harm reduction. In addition, future research should continue to explore 
the interaction between SDoH adversity and other individual factors hypothesized to impact 
reducing alcohol use.

Introduction 
 
It is estimated that about one-third of adults in the United States will meet criteria for 
alcohol use disorder (AUD) at some point in their lives (Grant et al., 2015). In addition, the 
National Survey on Drug Use and Health found that among people aged twelve or older in 2022, 
44.5% of individuals who consumed alcohol reported heavy alcohol use in the past month 
(SAMHSA, 2023). The National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) defined 
heavy alcohol use, or heavy drinking, as four or more standard drinks on any day or eight or 
more drinks per week for women and five or more drinks a day or fifteen or more a week for 
men (NIAAA, 2023a). Heavy alcohol use has been associated with increased involvement in 
accidents such as falling, alcohol poisoning, and chronic conditions (e.g., heart disease, liver 
disease, and stroke; CDC, 2022). Further, alcohol consumption is a leading cause of preventable 
deaths in the U.S., causing approximately 140,000 fatalities annually (NIAAA, 2023b). On 
January 3rd, 2025, the U.S. Surgeon General published a report identifying alcohol consumption 
as a leading preventable cause of cancer and calling for the addition of cancer warning labels on 
alcohol products (Office of the Surgeon General, 2025). 
With these potential consequences in mind, it is understandable that 46% of surveyed 
individuals who consume alcohol report attempting to reduce the quantity of alcohol they drink 
(Venlinx, 2022). Over the past decade, U.S. adults have increasingly participated in temporary 
alcohol abstinence challenges (TACCs) such as "Dry January" and "Sober October" (Siconolfi et 
al., 2024). The trend initially began in Europe during the early 2010s and has quickly gained 
popularity among individuals who were concerned about potential negative health outcomes 
from alcohol or wanted to evaluate their relationship with alcohol (de Visser et al., 2016; Field et 
al., 2020). Many individuals who participate in and complete TAACs report experiencing 

2 
 
positive changes in their health (i.e., sleep, weight loss, energy), feel more confident in their 
ability to control their alcohol intake, and continue to drink less alcohol less often even after the 
challenge ends (de Ternay et al., 2022; de Visser & Nicholls, 2020). 
 
Traditionally, recovery from alcohol use referred to individuals abstaining from alcohol 
use (i.e., not drinking any alcohol). Beginning in the 1700's as "sobriety circles" across Native 
American tribes, the sobriety and abstinence movement gained momentum (White, 2003; e.g., 
Washington Total Abstinence Society, the founding of Alcoholics Anonymous, Women for 
Sobriety). The narrative that abstinence was the only path to recovery from alcohol use was 
quickly written as treatment centers utilizing abstinence models (e.g., Minnesota Model of 
treatment) became the norm for treatment of alcohol use disorders (Stinchfield & Owen, 1998). 
However, not everyone who wants to reduce their alcohol use has the goal of abstaining. There is 
a growing body of research demonstrating the importance of recognizing that individuals who 
decide to change their drinking may have different endpoints in mind (Sliedrecht et al., 2022; 
Witkiewitz & Tucker, 2020). Furthermore, public policy and public opinion have become 
increasingly accepting of recovery as a spectrum (Hagman et al., 2022; Henssler et al., 2020). 
Individuals who identify as in recovery from alcohol use disorder (AUD) and those who do not 
may have made reductions in their alcohol use that can be categorized along a spectrum (e.g., 
moderation, abstinence, non-abstinent recovery; Eddie et al., 2022; Hagman et al., 2022). 
NIAAA (2023a) has published low-risk drinking guidelines, which state that women should not 
consume more than three drinks per day and no more than seven drinks per week. For men, this 
guideline is no more than four drinks per day and no more than fourteen drinks per week 
(NIAAA, 2023a). These recommendations can serve as limits for individuals who wish to reduce 
the quantity of their alcohol consumption without abstaining from alcohol altogether. For 

3 
 
example, a person who has previously struggled with heavy alcohol use may practice moderation 
by limiting their alcohol intake to one drink a day or no more than five a week, which is 
considered low-risk alcohol use. Another individual may identify with non-abstinent recovery 
and may choose not to drink alcohol but choose to smoke cannabis occasionally. It is important 
to note that recovery and reducing substance use are personal processes. As such, it is important 
to include individual recovery goals beyond abstinence. 
 
Despite the prevalence of risky alcohol use and the large proportion of people who report 
a desire to cut down, very few individuals who consume alcohol ever enter treatment. Even so, 
there is evidence that individuals diagnosed with AUD and individuals without an AUD 
diagnosis both naturally decrease their heavy drinking over time (Witkiewitz et al., 2014). It is 
unclear why individuals not diagnosed with AUD also decrease their alcohol consumption over 
time; however, researchers note it is likely due to a combination of neurobiological, 
psychological, social, and environmental factors (Witkiewitz et al., 2019). Previous research has 
endorsed the importance of several individual (e.g., self-efficacy; Shaw & DiClemente, 2016; 
help-seeking behaviors; Gilbert et al., 2019; stress; Sliedrecht et al., 2019) and environmental 
factors (e.g., crime and neighborhood disadvantage; Mennis et al., 2016; racism; Gibbons et al., 
2012; alcohol availability; Karriker-Jaffe et al., 2018) that likely impact reducing alcohol use. 
For example, an individual with low self-efficacy and low help-seeking, living in an area with 
higher alcohol availability and neighborhood disadvantage, may struggle with reducing their 
alcohol use compared to another individual with fewer risk factors and less adversity. This study 
focused on the impact of the individual factor of self-efficacy, as it is an individual factor 
consistently associated with substance use outcomes. 
Factors Impacting Alcohol Use 

4 
 
 
The interaction of individual and environmental factors is illustrated within the socialecological model. The social-ecological model proposes that individuals exist within their 
microsystem (i.e., family, school, peers, support), nested within their community (i.e., norms, 
attitudes), and the larger macro-level (i.e., policies, systemic racism; Bronfenbrenner, 1994). 
Given that individual factors are nested within the individual's environment, larger systems (e.g., 
social determinants of health) likely impact alcohol use. Social determinants of health (SDoH) 
may be associated with reductions in, or lack of reductions in, heavy alcohol consumption 
(Porthé et al., 2021; Swan et al., 2021). According to Healthy People 2030, SDoH refers to the 
conditions of an individual's environment that affect health, including quality-of-life outcomes 
and risks (ODPHP, n.d.). SDoH is theorized to consist of five main domains: Economic Stability, 
Education, Health and Healthcare, Neighborhood and Built Environment, and Social and 
Community Context (Elias et al., 2019). Together, these domains of SDoH have been connected 
to significant health disparities, including physical health conditions, mental health, and 
substance use (Cockerham et al., 2017; Matus et al., 2024; ODPHP, n.d.). 
Economic Stability 
 
Economic stability refers to financial resources and factors that impact the individual's 
context, including employment status, food insecurity, housing stability, and poverty (Elias et al., 
2019; ODPHP, n.d.). Further, employment instability, conditions that impact an individual's 
ability to work, and difficulty affording basic needs all impact health outcomes. Relatedly, 
struggling to pay for food, housing, health care, and education limits individuals' access to 
improving their health and well-being (ODPHP, n.d.). Experiencing adversity in the economic 
stability domain is associated with more heavy alcohol consumption (Cerdá et al., 2010; Collins, 
2016). For example, Glass and colleagues (2017) interviewed 21,410 participants who consumed 

5 
 
alcohol at baseline and after three years. They found that poverty predicted greater heavy alcohol 
consumption at follow-up (Glass et al., 2017). This effect was stronger among Latine and Black 
individuals, with Black individuals experiencing the highest risk ratio compared to White 
individuals (Glass et al., 2017), illustrating the intersectional effects of race and socioeconomic 
standing. Given the direct relationship between access to education and elements of the 
economic stability domain (e.g., housing stability and poverty), the education and economic 
stability domains are closely related. 
Education 
 
The education domain includes access to early childhood education, enrollment in higher 
education, high school graduation, and literacy in the dominant language (i.e., English; ODPHP, 
n.d.). Research has documented that people with higher levels of education are more likely to 
live longer, healthier lives (ODPHP, n.d.; Shiels et al., 2017). This begins with access to early 
childhood education and continues as the individual completes educational milestones (e.g., high 
school graduation, GED, undergraduate graduation; Zajacova & Lawrence, 2018). Access to 
schools and resources that support educational development and achievement is often segregated 
or stratified by income (Zajacova & Lawrence, 2018). Schools located in areas with lower 
property values, often with higher concentrations of people of color and low-income residents, 
often cannot afford the same resources as schools in more affluent areas. This makes the school 
appear to be a "poor performing school," which means it will receive less financial support from 
the state, thereby exacerbating disparities and perpetuating the cycle of poverty and health 
inequities (Zajacova & Lawrence, 2018). Even after high school or GED completion, some 
people cannot afford college for reasons beyond their control (e.g., needing a job to support their 
family, student debt being daunting). High school completion and educational attainment are 

6 
 
negatively associated with heavy alcohol use and alcohol use disorder (Crum et al., 1993; Rosoff 
et al., 2021). Education directly impacts access to careers that may provide better insurance 
coverage. Therefore, education is also tied to the domain of health and healthcare. 
Health and Healthcare 
 
Health and healthcare include an individual's perceived access to healthcare, access to 
primary care, and health literacy (ODPHP, n.d.). People may not receive the health care services 
they need for many reasons, including lack of health insurance or being underinsured, cost of 
services, not having a primary care provider (PCP), distance from health facilities, or 
unfamiliarity with the health care system. PCPs help individuals receive preventative care, health 
screenings, and treatment. Therefore, when someone does not have an assigned PCP, they often 
do not get the recommended services, which can lead to the deterioration of their health (Shi, 
2012). Further, low health literacy is associated with heavy alcohol use and AUD (Chisolm et al., 
2014; Rolova et al., 2020). This relation may be due to other negative economic, social, physical, 
and mental health factors (Rolova et al., 2021). Perceived access and distance from health 
facilities significantly impact healthcare utilization (Cerletti et al., 2021). Consequently, an 
individual's physical environment is connected to health and healthcare. 
Neighborhood and Built Environment 
 
The neighborhood and built environment domain includes access to healthy foods, crime 
and violence, environmental conditions, and housing quality (Cockerham et al., 2017; OHPDP, 
n.d.). The built environment is reflected in access to green and recreational spaces, public transit 
and crosswalks, and pollution (e.g., sound, light, air; Keralis et al., 2020; OHPDP, n.d.). People 
of color and low-income families experience a higher likelihood of risk associated with the 
neighborhood and built environment, which can impact overall health (e.g., air quality, noise 

7 
 
pollution; Evans & Kantrowitz, 2002). In terms of alcohol use, the different components of the 
built environment have been negatively associated with heavy alcohol consumption (Bernstein et 
al., 2007; Jitnarin et al., 2015; Pollack et al., 2005). Meaning, more protective factors (e.g., 
access to healthy food, lower crime rates, better environmental conditions) are associated with 
less heavy alcohol consumption. The neighborhood and built environment domain describes the 
physical space individuals live in, and the social and community context domain is within this 
space. 
Social and Community Context 
 
The final domain, social and community context, includes civic participation, experiences 
of discrimination, incarceration, and social cohesion. Humans are inherently social beings; 
interpersonal stressors can significantly impact an individual's health. Research has connected 
experiences of discrimination (i.e., microaggressions, bullying, harassment) with significant 
negative health experiences (e.g., hypertension, birth outcomes, heart disease; Cockerham et al., 
2017; Paradies, 2006). More adversity in the social and community context domain is related to 
heavy alcohol use (Collins, 2016; Roche et al., 2015; Sudhinaraset et al., 2016). Further, 
systemic barriers such as voter suppression, over-policing, and the lack of representation of 
marginalized individuals in positions of power can disempower individuals and worsen health 
outcomes (Theall et al., 2022; Yagoda, 2019). 
The results of studies referenced above demonstrate the significance of SDoH and 
reducing alcohol use. Nevertheless, it is important to note that some research on SDoH and 
heavy alcohol consumption has reported inconclusive or non-significant findings. A systematic 
review of community-level social factors and alcohol use, completed by Bryden and colleagues 
(2013), identified some of these inconsistencies. Overall, the studies they reviewed supported 

8 
 
social capital as a protective factor. However, findings on the impact of alcohol consumption 
among low socio-economic status (SES) compared to high SES were inconclusive, with some 
studies concluding higher SES with greater heavy alcohol consumption and some studies finding 
non-significant differences between groups (Byrden et al., 2013). These inconclusive findings 
regarding SES may be attributable to confounding third variables, given the multidimensional 
and intersecting nature of SDoH. 
 
Naturally, these domains are interconnected and do not exist in a vacuum. Advantages or 
disadvantages in one area may be closely related to those in another, thereby amplifying their 
effects. For example, individuals may not have an assigned PCP because their job does not 
provide health insurance benefits. Previous research across multiple SDoH domains has yielded 
similar findings. In a sample of Canadian women living with HIV, researchers found that 
participants with more SDoH adversity - or fewer resources/protective SDoH factors - reported 
more frequent heavy alcohol consumption (Shokoohi et al., 2019). Similarly, a study in Portugal 
that randomly sampled residents of an urban region found that participants with high alcohol 
intake were more likely to be less educated, work in blue-collar occupations, and consume fewer 
than five servings of fruit or vegetables per day (Dias et al., 2011). High-intake alcohol 
consumption was defined using the limits set by the American Heart Association: greater than 15 
grams of alcohol a day for females and greater than 30 grams for males (Dias et al., 2011); these 
are similar to the guidelines suggested by NIAAA. SDoH provides a picture of individuals' 
environment and has a powerful influence on alcohol use. 
The Role of Social Determinants of Health and Reducing Alcohol Use 
Given the impact of SDoH on heavy alcohol consumption, it is likely that SDoH is also 
connected to reducing alcohol use. Previous research on SDoH and reducing alcohol use has 

9 
 
focused on individual aspects of SDoH instead of the construct as a whole. SDoH is 
multidimensional, and its domains are interconnected; therefore, the influence of SDoH on 
reducing alcohol use should be assessed as a whole rather than in pieces, as seen in previous 
research. 
There is a growing body of evidence supporting aspects of SDoH domains and their 
impact on reducing alcohol use, including housing (Jason et al., 2021; Karriker-Jaffe et al., 2018; 
Martinelli et al., 2020; Pauly et al., 2019), unemployment (Eddie et al., 2020; Karriker-Jaffe et 
al., 2018), poverty (Karriker-Jaffe et al., 2018; Rhew et al., 2020), education (Eddie et al., 2020; 
Lanham & Tirado, 2011; Slater et al., 2017), crime (Martinelli et al., 2020; Rhew et al., 2020), 
social context (Rhew et al., 2020), and discrimination (Mak et al., 2014; Slater et al., 2017). 
These studies support the impact of adversity in parts of SDoH on individuals attempting to 
reduce their alcohol use. However, many of these studies are limited by racial/ethnic 
homogeneity and by surveying only participants involved in treatment. Further, many of these 
studies measured alcohol use outcomes by abstinence instead of considering recovery as a 
spectrum (e.g., moderation, harm reduction). This may be due to the treatment setting of the 
sample; given the support for recovery as a spectrum (Eddie et al., 2022; Henssler et al., 2021), 
abstinence-only outcomes are a significant limitation of existing research. 
Previous research also supports SDoH factors impacting individuals who have made 
changes to their drinking. Gilbert and colleagues (2021) surveyed a representative sample of U.S. 
adults in recovery or with a resolved alcohol use disorder during the COVID-19 pandemic. They 
found education (e.g., high school diploma, some college) to be protective, and poverty status 
was associated with an increased risk of mild relapse among the surveyed men (Gilbert et al., 
2021). Among the women surveyed, they found that being out of the labor force (e.g., retired, 

10 
 
student, disabled/unable to work, homemaker) was a risk factor for mild relapse (Gilbert et al., 
2021). This study is one of the few that made significant efforts to recruit a diverse sample (i.e., 
oversampling participants of color) and included a spectrum of recovery (e.g., moderation, 
abstinence, harm reduction; Gilbert et al., 2021), both major strengths of the study. Nevertheless, 
the design considered individual factors within the education and economic stability domains 
rather than the full impact of SDoH on recovery maintenance. It is important to examine SDoH 
as a whole construct because the domains are interconnected according to theory (Elias et al., 
2019). 
A study following clients discharged from detoxification, residential, and outpatient 
substance use treatment in California from 1995-2006 tested the socioecological model and 
alcohol recovery group membership using latent class analyses (Karriker-Jaffe et al., 2020). The 
results supported the importance of the built environment (i.e., the density of liquor stores and 
bars), economic (i.e., poverty), and social context (i.e., social support, involvement) on recovery 
group membership (Karriker-Jaffe et al., 2020). Specifically, individuals in the 
"Relapsing/Rising" class were more likely to live in an area with high alcohol access, experience 
poverty, and be less involved with recovery-supporting peers and activities (Karriker-Jaffe et al., 
2020). Although this study included the impact of individual factors (i.e., comorbid psychiatric 
severity, return to treatment) on recovery group membership, the researchers did not evaluate 
self-efficacy, which is a significant predictor of alcohol use outcomes (DiBello et al., 2019). 
Nevertheless, this study aided in establishing support for the socioecological model to predict 
reductions in alcohol use by evaluating parts of SDoH in a diverse treatment sample. 
Swan and colleagues (2021) found that economic stability, health care access and quality, 
education, and poverty impacted recovery group membership (i.e., (1)Low Functioning/Frequent 

11 
 
Heavy Drinking, (2) Low Functioning/Infrequent Heavy Drinking, (3) High Functioning/Heavy 
Drinking, (4) High Functioning/Infrequent Drinking) 3-years after treatment for AUD. 
Specifically, income above the poverty level predicted group membership of Profile 4 (High 
Functioning/Infrequent Drinking) compared to Profile 2 (Low Functioning/Infrequent Drinking; 
Swan et al., 2021). Similarly, lower income inequality predicted membership of Profile 4 
compared to Profile 1 (Low Functioning/Frequent Heavy Drinking), and higher income 
inequality predicted membership of Profile 4 compared to Profile 2 (Swan et al., 2021). Lastly, 
higher insured and lower income inequality predicted group membership of Profile 3 (High 
Functioning/Heavy Drinking) compared to Profile 1 (Swan et al., 2021). To summarize, less 
SDoH adversity predicted membership in the higher-functioning recovery group. This study is 
significant as one of the first to document the effects of multiple SDoH domains on nonabstinent recovery. Nevertheless, this study is limited by racial/ethnic homogeneity and the use 
of a clinical sample, similar to other studies reviewed above. Given that many individuals 
struggling with heavy alcohol use will never enter treatment, it is important to understand how 
SDoH adversity impacts non-treatment-seeking people's ability to reduce their alcohol use. 
Individual-Level Influences Within the Socioecological Model 
Although these studies established a connection between SDoH and reduced alcohol use, 
they did not account for individual-level influences incorporated into the socioecological model. 
Specifically, they are missing self-efficacy, which has been established as an important predictor 
of alcohol use outcomes (abstinence at follow-up; Ilgen et al., 2005; consequences; DiBello et 
al., 2019). Previous research on self-efficacy and substance use has consistently demonstrated 
self-efficacy's protective impact on substance use outcomes (e.g., return to use; Hayaki et al., 

12 
 
2011; Kadden & Litt, 2011; McKellar et al., 2008; abstinence; Ilgen et al., 2005; McKellar et al., 
2008; Vielva & Iraugi, 2002). 
Bandura conceptualized self-efficacy as an individual's expectations of their efficacy as 
informed by performance accomplishments, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and 
psychological states (Bandura, 1977). According to the cognitive-behavioral model of substance 
use relapse, self-efficacy is the individual's perceived ability to manage high-risk situations and 
rely on coping skills (Marlatt & Gordon, 1985; Marlatt et al., 1995). As it relates to substance 
use, efficacy beliefs were categorized by Marlatt and colleagues (1995) into the following five 
categories: (1) resistance self-efficacy, (2) harm-reduction self-efficacy, (3) action self-efficacy, 
(4) coping self-efficacy, and (5) recovery self-efficacy. First, resistance self-efficacy refers to an 
individual's belief in their ability to avoid substance use before initiating it (Marlatt et al., 1995). 
Second, harm-reduction self-efficacy refers to an individual's belief in their ability to reduce 
their substance use risk (Marlatt et al., 1995). Third, action self-efficacy refers to the individual's 
initiation of changing their substance use and belief in their ability to achieve their goal (e.g., 
abstinence, controlled use; Marlatt et al., 1995). Fourth, coping self-efficacy refers to the 
individual's belief in their ability to cope in high-risk situations and avoid returning to use 
(Marlatt et al., 1995). The fifth, recovery self-efficacy, occurs when an individual has maintained 
their recovery (e.g., abstinence, moderation, etc.) and their belief in their ability to continue to 
cope and recover from lapses or returning to use (Marlatt et al., 1995). 
Most of the research on substance use treatment and self-efficacy focuses on the fourth 
type, coping self-efficacy, where researchers are interested in how confident the participants are 
in their ability to cope and maintain their progress; this study also focuses on coping selfefficacy. Given the evidence supporting the influence of macro-level environmental factors (i.e., 

13 
 
SDoH) on reducing alcohol use, research testing the socioecological model should continue to 
explore the impact of individual factors (i.e., self-efficacy) on this relation. 
The Role of Self-efficacy and Intentions to Reduce Alcohol Use 
Initially, research questions were primarily viewed through the lens of abstinence 
(Kadden & Litt, 2011). However, in line with the broader substance use field, there has been a 
growing focus on examining the effects of self-efficacy from a harm reduction perspective. It is 
important to note that abstinence is also considered a form of harm reduction. Both reducing and 
abstaining from use focus on decreasing and maintaining this change. Since the harm reduction 
umbrella includes abstinence, previous research focusing on abstinence can be utilized to inform 
future research. 
Abstinence research found that abstinence self-efficacy directly predicts alcohol quantity, 
frequency, and consequences among individuals in alcohol treatment programs (Maisto et al., 
2000). Within their sample of former treatment clients, Maisto and colleagues (2000) found a 
significant negative direct effect of self-efficacy on their outcomes as self-efficacy increased, 
alcohol quantity, frequency, and consequences decreased. In another study, one year after 
treatment discharge, Ilgen and colleagues (2005) found that high levels of abstinence selfefficacy were the strongest predictor of abstinence at follow-up. Further, self-efficacy has been 
found to mediate the relation between attitude toward limiting drinking and alcohol 
use/consequences over five months among college students mandated to attend treatment after a 
campus alcohol violation (DiBello et al., 2019). This means self-efficacy was associated with 
reduced alcohol quantity and related problems (DiBello et al., 2019). 
In terms of heavy alcohol consumption, higher self-efficacy plays a significant role in 
reducing the number of heavy alcohol consumption episodes over three months among a young 

14 
 
adult sample who reported past-month heavy alcohol consumption (Blume et al., 2003). While 
this study was intended to be a community sample, most young adults surveyed were White 
college students. Another non-clinical sample revealed that participants with higher self-efficacy 
generally decreased their alcohol intake, though they did not necessarily abstain from alcohol, 
unlike previous studies. Kraus and colleagues (2013) found that self-efficacy in reducing 
drinking was negatively associated with recent heavy alcohol consumption. Although this 
college student sample was racially homogenous, the findings are significant because they 
demonstrate that self-efficacy can be protective for heavy alcohol consumption for individuals 
who desire to reduce their alcohol use without necessarily abstaining. Given the evidence 
supporting the importance of individual (self-efficacy) and community factors (SDoH) on 
alcohol use, it is important to understand how the two work together, specifically within a 
diverse, adult, non-clinical sample. 
Present Study 
Considering the findings above, there are several gaps in the science of examining the 
impact of the interaction of macro factors (i.e., SDoH) and individual (i.e., self-efficacy) on 
reducing alcohol use in diverse non-clinical samples (Collins, 2016; Sudhinaraset et al., 2016; 
Swan et al., 2021). Further, research on recovery as a spectrum, including individuals who may 
not be seen for treatment (Witkiewitz et al., 2014), is limited. A handful of studies on reducing 
alcohol use have recruited a racially heterogeneous sample, tested pieces of SDoH, and 
established self-efficacy in cutting down alcohol use beyond abstinence (e.g., moderation, harm 
reduction). Nevertheless, participants of color who likely experience more negative effects of 
SDoH adversity (Glass et al., 2017; Mak et al., 2014) and alcohol use consequences (Karriker-
Jaffe et al., 2012; Zapolski et al., 2014) continue to be undersampled in research. Finally, the full 

15 
 
impact of SDoH has not been fully tested (i.e., SDoH encompassing all theorized domains; Swan 
et al., 2021; Witkiewitz et al., 2019). This study was designed to address these gaps by recruiting 
a population that is diverse or likely to experience health disparities to answer the following 
question: How is SDoH adversity related to the likelihood of reporting reduced alcohol use, and 
to what extent does self-efficacy moderate this relationship? 
 
This study tested whether self-efficacy for cutting down on drinking moderates the 
association between social determinants of health adversity and alcohol use, as measured by 
perceived changes in alcohol use. It was hypothesized that: 
H1: Individuals experiencing fewer social determinants of health adversity will be more likely to 
report reducing their alcohol consumption. 
H2: Self-efficacy in reducing alcohol consumption will moderate the relation between social 
determinants of health adversity and perceived changes in alcohol use. The interaction between 
social determinants of health adversity and self-efficacy will be negatively associated with 
perceived changes in alcohol use; the interaction will reduce the effect of social determinants of 
health on alcohol use. 
Method 
Participants 
 
An a priori power analysis was run using the free software G*Power Version 3.1.9.4 
(Faul et al., 2009) to calculate statistical power and sample size. Results of a logistic regression, 
one-tailed, probability of H1 = 0.12, probability of H0 = 0.06, alpha at 0.05, normally distributed, 
and moderate association R-square = 0.25 suggested a sample size of 228 with an estimated 
power of .80. The probabilities, associations, and power were informed by previous research on 
logistic regression (Williamson, n.d.) and SDoH on alcohol use (Dias et al., 2011; Shokoohi et 

16 
 
al., 2019). However, due to concerns of fraudulent responses (e.g., bots, participants taking the 
survey multiple times), a multivariate outlier, and missing data, the final sample size used for 
analyses was n = 177. Please see the Procedure and Preliminary Analyses sections for more 
information. 
Participants were adults (Mage = 29.92, SD = 8.15) who had recently changed or were 
planning to change their drinking and were not currently in substance use treatment. The sample 
was diverse, n = 101 (57.06%) identified as a person of color, n = 10 (5.68%) identified as 
transgender and/or gender expansive, n = 44 (24.86%) identified as 2SLGBQIA+, n = 77 or 
43.50% living with a disability, n = 109 (61.58%) identified as "in recovery", and the median 
reported gross annual income was $40,000 (IQR = $95,500) and ranged from $0 - $1,200,000 
(M = $72,782.85, SD = $13,5751.29). Regarding the reported reduction in alcohol use, n = 148 
(83.62%) of participants reported drinking less alcohol since deciding to make changes to their 
alcohol use. Participants who reported stopping their alcohol use (n = 77) endorsed an average of 
2.06 years (SD = 2.94) of abstinence from alcohol. Please see the sample descriptives in Table 1. 
Procedure 
 
Participants were recruited via flyers posted around local spaces around the New York 
capital region (i.e., libraries, churches, coffee shops, community centers) and listservs/social 
media pages associated with substance use. Physical locations were identified using the Area 
Deprivation Index (ADI; Kind & Buckingham, 2018; University of Wisconsin School of 
Medicine Public Health, 2021). The ADI is based on the measure created by the Health 
Resources & Services Administration and adapted by a University of Wisconsin research team to 
measure and map neighborhood disadvantage (e.g., income, education, employment, housing 
quality) at a state or national level (Kind & Buckingham, 2018; University of Wisconsin School 

17 
 
of Medicine Public Health, 2021). Flyers were hung around capital-region communities with 
neighborhood disadvantage one standard deviation above the national average within a 25-mile 
radius of Albany, NY. These spaces were selected because they serve a broad range of 
individuals who are likely diverse in their identities, access to resources, and experiences with 
health disparities, particularly in areas with large populations of Black and Latine individuals. 
 
Participants were eligible to participate if they were (1) between 18 and 65 years old, (2) 
wanted to or were currently reducing their alcohol consumption, (3) not currently attending 
treatment for a substance use disorder, and (4) had access to an electronic device to take the 
survey. Participants were not eligible and excluded if they (1) were outside of the listed age 
range, (2) not interested in reducing their alcohol consumption, (3) currently attending substance 
use treatment, (4) did not have access to an electronic device to take the survey, or (5) were not 
able to read English. When participants followed the survey link, they saw the informed consent 
form (See Appendix A), which included information about resources if anything in the survey 
caused them discomfort or distress. Participants completed the Protocol for Responding to and 
Assessing Patients' Assets, Risks, and Experiences (PRAPARE; NACHC, 2019) which 
measured SDoH adversity (the independent variable), the Alcohol Reduction Strategies Current 
Confidence Questionnaire (ARS-CC; Kraus et al., 2012) which measured self-efficacy of cutting 
down on alcohol use (the moderator), the Typical Atypical Drinking Diary (TADD; Hogan, 
2005) primed participants to estimate their alcohol consumption looked like before they decided 
to reduce their drinking, then identified if their current alcohol consumption is "less" or "the 
same or more" compared to when they decided to cut down (the dependent variable), and a short 
demographics measure. 

18 
 
Counterbalancing was used to control for order effects by using Qualtrics' randomization 
function. After completing the questionnaires, participants were presented with the debriefing 
information on the last page, including previous resources in the informed consent form. There 
were also links to local resources, including food pantries, transportation, and health insurance 
assistance. Finally, a link directed participants to a separate form in which they entered their 
email address to receive a $5 e-gift card as a thank-you for their participation. This information 
was kept separate from their data. Gift cards were paid via Tremendous, an incentive payment 
company used for research. The participants chose their preferred retailer gift card (i.e., Amazon, 
Walmart, Starbucks). 
In December 2024, this researcher discontinued recruiting participants via Facebook 
groups after Qualtrics flagged 30 of 190 collected responses as potentially fraudulent (e.g., bots 
or duplicate responses). This researcher resumed data collection via physical flyers and email 
listservs in January 2025 after receiving IRB approval for two attention-check questions and 
additional logic branching to remove potentially fraudulent responses automatically. 
Measures 
Social Determinants of Health 
Social determinants of health was measured using the Protocol for Responding to and 
Assessing Patients' Assets, Risks, and Experiences (PRAPARE; NACHC, 2019; See Appendix B) 
risk tally score. The PRAPARE is a 20-item tool for screening SDoH adversity based on the five 
theorized domains (Elias et al., 2019; ODPHP, n.d.). Similar to other SDoH measures, 
PRAPARE was initially developed for use in clinical settings (Elias et al., 2019; NACHC, 2019). 
Due to its theory-driven development and acceptable reliability, PRAPARE is widely used in 
SDoH research, particularly for its risk tally score. The PRAPARE risk tally score measured 

19 
 
SDoH adversity by summing the number of distinct risks (tallies) endorsed by the participant. 
Seventeen items were dichotomously measured (i.e., 1 - risk present or 0 - absent), one item 
measured transportation adversity up to two tallies (i.e., 1 - yes, medical; yes, non-medical, or 0 
- no needs), and one item measured material security (e.g., clothing, utilities) one tally each for 
up to seven tallies. Two items were used to produce a single tally of poverty. Participants 
estimated their annual income and reported their household size. Unfortunately, the item 
prompting participants to indicate their household size was accidentally omitted from the survey. 
Therefore, the poverty experience was based on the 2024 Federal Poverty Level (FPL) guidelines 
at a household size = 1. Risk tally scores for this sample ranged from 0 to 19, with higher scores 
indicating greater SDoH risk factors or SDoH adversity. Previous studies using the PRAPARE 
risk tally score among community residents with heart failure (M = 4.9, SD = 2.8) and adult 
outpatient community mental health center clients (M = 4.99, SD = 2.71) have indicated 
acceptable internal consistency, Cronbach's α = 0.86 (Luzius et al., 2022; NACHC, 2021; Osei 
Bach et al., 2023). This study's sample (M = 6.88, SD = 5.15) showed acceptable internal 
consistency, with Cronbach's α = 0.78. 
Self-Efficacy for Cutting Down 
The Alcohol Reduction Strategies-Current Confidence Questionnaire (ARS-CC; Kraus et 
al., 2012; see Appendix C) is a 31-item measure that assesses an individual's confidence (selfefficacy) in drinking-reduction self-control skills. Participants rated items using a five-point 
Likert-type scale (1 = Not at all confident, 2 = A little confident, 3 = Moderately confident, 4 = 
Very confident, 5 = Completely confident). Scores were determined by averaging the endorsed 
strategies. If a reduction strategy did not apply to a participant, it was coded as 0 Does not apply, 
and not included in the average. Higher scores indicated higher self-efficacy. Unexpectedly, n = 

20 
 
15 participants endorsed "Does not apply" for all ARS-CC items. Analyses were conducted with 
and without this group (coded as 0 = "Does not apply" for analyses), and the results were 
similar; therefore, these 15 participants were included in the final analysis. Previous studies, 
including university (M = 3.6, SD = 1.22) and adult community samples (M = 3.0, SD = 1.1), 
have indicated acceptable internal consistency, Cronbach's α = 0.96 (DiBello et al., 2019; 
Hoffmann et al., 2013; Kraus et al., 2012). This study's sample (M = 3.00, SD = 1.34), internal 
consistency was acceptable, with Cronbach's α = 0.98. 
Perceived Changes in Alcohol Consumption 
Participants were asked to estimate their alcohol consumption before they decided to 
reduce their drinking (pre-change) and then indicated if they consumed the same or less alcohol 
in the past week (See Appendix D). The pre-change alcohol consumption estimate served to 
prime the participants to consider their previous drinking before indicating if they perceived their 
alcohol consumption to have decreased or stayed the same. The Typical Atypical Drinking Diary 
(TADD; Hogan, 2005; See Appendix D) retrospectively estimated participants' previous alcohol 
consumption and drinking patterns before deciding to reduce their drinking (pre-change). The 
six-item TADD included one dichotomous item asking if participants currently consume alcohol 
(e.g., yes or no), a chart of common beverages with alcohol for participants to select which 
drinks they typically consume, two weekly diaries, and two items measuring the frequency of 
typical vs. atypical drinking (Hogan, 2005; Patterson et al., 2019). If participants indicated they 
no longer drink alcohol, they were prompted to estimate the date they stopped drinking before 
proceeding to the common beverage chart. Participants then completed two weekly diaries, one 
for typical and one for atypical weeks (i.e., weeks with heavier or lighter drinking; Hogan, 2005). 
The diaries were recorded in a provided table (See Appendix C), comprising seven rows for each 

21 
 
day of the week and five columns for beverage type, alcohol by volume (ABV) percentage, total 
amount consumed, start time, and finish time (Hogan, 2005). Participants could refer to the 
common beverage chart to estimate alcohol consumption. After each drinking diary, participants 
estimated the number of weeks during which they drank the typical or atypical amount over the 
specified period. Although the TADD was not included in analyses and mainly served to prime 
the participants to think about their previous drinking patterns, previous research comparing 
retrospective alcohol use measures found the TADD provided a more accurate estimate for total 
alcohol consumed, number of drinking days, and number of heavy alcohol consumption days 
compared to other measures (e.g., daily drinking diary, timeline follow back; Patterson et al., 
2019). Previous studies using the TADD for heavy drinking days among college student samples 
(M = 4.86, SD = 4.29) and post-COVID-19 change in heavy drinking days among adult 
community samples (M = 0.21, SD = 1.5) have indicated acceptable internal consistency, 
Cronbach's α = 0.78 (Clay et al., 2023; Patterson et al., 2019). This study's sample estimated an 
average of 2.39 heavy drinking days pre-change (M = 2.39, SD = 3.22). Since items represent the 
number of drinks and time spent on alcohol for typical and atypical weeks rather than 
interchangeable indicators of a latent construct, internal consistency was not calculated in this 
study. Further, due to being a retrospective diary, it is difficult to determine intraclass correlation without a measure of current alcohol use. The TADD was not analyzed or 
included in analyses. Participants then completed one item on perceived reductions in alcohol 
use, "Compared to my drinking before, I am drinking... (1 = less, 0 = same or more). 
Demographic Questionnaire 
Participants were also asked to complete a demographic questionnaire. Participants were 
asked to indicate their gender identity, sex assigned at birth, sexual orientation, age, racial 

22 
 
identity, disability status, income, employment status, higher education status, participation in 
the 12-step/abstinence model, recovery identity, readiness to change, and previous substance use 
treatment (See Appendix E). 
Attention Checks 
 
Two attention-check questions were included after January 2025 as an additional layer to 
filter out potentially fraudulent responses (See Appendix F). Given prior research on the 
potential for false positives in attention check questions (Muszyński, 2023), responses were 
excluded only if both attention checks failed. At the conclusion of data collection, none of the 
participants missed both attention check questions; therefore, no participants were excluded. 
Results 
Preliminary Analyses 
 
SPSS was used to test all assumptions for regression analysis. Results of Little's Missing 
Completely at Random test (MCAR; Little, 1988) were non-significant (p > .05); therefore, 
values were considered missing completely at random. Forty-four participants were excluded 
because more than 20% of their values were missing. The Expectation-Maximization (E.M.) 
option in SPSS was used to impute missing values for participants with less than 20% missing 
values. One participant was excluded as a multivariate outlier using the Mahalanobis distance 
test (Dupuis et al., 2019). The assumption of linearity was evaluated using bivariate scatter plots 
of continuous variables and the logit scores to confirm a linear relationship. Multicollinearity 
was assessed by the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF); all VIFs were below 5. 
Descriptive Statistics 
 
Regarding SDoH adversity, participants reported an average of 6.88 risk tallies (SD = 
5.15) on the PRAPARE measure, indicating that this sample experienced greater SDoH adversity 

23 
 
than previous community samples (Luzius et al., 2022; Osei Bach et al., 2023). A PRAPARE 
risk tally score greater than or equal to 5 is considered high risk for SDoH adversity (Nain et al., 
2024; Weir et al., 2020). In this sample, n = 112 (63.28%) of participants scored greater than or 
equal to 5 on the PRAPARE risk tally and are considered at high risk of experiencing negative 
impacts from SDoH adversity, compared with individuals with greater access to resources and 
lower risk tally scores. The most common personal characteristic was participants identifying as 
people of color (n = 101, 57.06%). The most common "risk tally" was employment (i.e., 
unemployed, part-time, or temporary work), n = 96 (54.24%). In terms of material insecurity 
(e.g., difficulty paying for or being unable to access food, clothing, housing), participants, on 
average, reported difficulty accessing 1.36 (SD = 1.47) material resources. Utilities (n = 54, 
30.51%) and medicine or healthcare (n = 54, 30.51%) were the most commonly reported sources 
of material insecurity. The most common social and emotional health risk was social integration, 
n = 83 (46.89%), indicating that participants see or talk to people they care about or feel close to 
(e.g., friends, family, attending clubs or gatherings) less than 3 times a week. The least common 
risk tally was English Proficiency: "Which language are you most comfortable speaking?" n = 3 
(1.69%). This result is likely due to the survey being only offered in English. 
 
For self-efficacy to reduce alcohol consumption, participants on average endorsed feeling 
"moderately confident" overall that they would use the listed strategies to reduce their drinking 
(M = 3.00, SD = 1.34), which is similar to previous research using the ARS-CC (Hoffman et al., 
2013; Kraus et al., 2012). Participants endorsed the highest confidence that they would use the 
strategy "Avoid catching up if you start drinking after others" (M = 3.28, SD = 1.64), indicating 
participants were on average "moderately confident" that they would use this strategy to reduce 
their alcohol use. The lowest confidence that they would use the "Keep track of each drink on 

24 
 
your cell phone or piece of paper" (M = 2.50, SD = 1.74), indicating participants were on 
average between "a little confident" and "moderately confident" that they would use this 
strategy to reduce their alcohol use. As mentioned in the participants section of the methods, the 
sample was surprisingly heterogeneous in their responses on the measure of self-efficacy. In 
addition to the participants who responded "Does not apply" to all items of the ARS-CC (n = 15, 
8.48%), n = 17 participants (9.60%) responded "Does not apply" to at least a third of the ARS-
CC items. On average, participants responded "Does not apply" to 5.37 items (SD = 10.42); 
most participants rarely endorsed "Does not apply" (Med = 0, IQR = 3). 
 
In terms of correlations, the highest correlation coefficient was between previous mutual 
aid and previous substance use treatment (φ = .60, p < .01), indicating a moderate positive 
correlation, meaning participants who previously attended mutual aid were more likely to also 
have previously engaged in substance use treatment. In addition, there was a moderate positive 
correlation between SDoH adversity and previous mutual aid (rpb = .46, p < .01), meaning 
participants who experience more SDoH adversity were more likely to be involved in mutual aid. 
Similarly, there was a moderate positive correlation between SDoH adversity and previous 
substance use treatment (rpb = .42, p < .01). In contrast, the lowest correlation coefficient was 
between previous mutual aid and readiness to change (rpb = -.15, p < .05), a weak negative 
correlation indicating participants with previous mutual aid involvement tend to report lower 
readiness to change. The second-lowest correlation coefficient was between identifying as "in 
recovery" and self-efficacy (rpb = -.15, p < .05), a weak negative correlation indicating that 
participants who identified as "in recovery" tended to report lower self-efficacy. 
Although gender was not significantly correlated with reporting reduced alcohol use (rpb 
= .06, p > .05), it was retained as a covariate in analyses testing the hypotheses, consistent with 

25 
 
previous alcohol use research (Cook et al., 2025; White, 2020). Similarly, although identifying as 
"in recovery" (φ = .36, p < .01), readiness to change (rpb = .24, p < .01), previous mutual aid (φ = 
-.21, p < .01), and previous substance use treatment (φ = -.25, p < .01) were weakly correlated 
with reporting reduced alcohol use, they were included as covariates in analyses consistent with 
theory and previous alcohol use research (recovery; Best et al., 2016; Day et al., 2023, readiness; 
Maisto et al., 2011; Schwebel et al., 2023, previous mutual aid; Chambers et al., 2017; White et 
al., 2003, previous substance use treatment; Leggio & Lee, 2017; Witkiewitz et al., 2019). Please 
see Table 2 for the full correlation matrix. 
Major Analyses 
 
Logistic regression with moderator analyses was run using PROCESS (Hayes, 2022) to 
test all hypotheses. The independent variable was total SDoH adversity scores (PRAPARE risk 
tally); the moderator was average self-efficacy (ARS-CC); and the dependent variable was 
perceived reductions in alcohol consumption. Gender, motivation to change, recovery identity, 
and previous substance use treatment were included as covariates. Logistic regression with 
moderation analyses tested the effect of SDoH adversity on the likelihood of perceiving 
reductions in alcohol consumption, moderated by self-efficacy for cutting down (Hayes, 2022). 
Identifying a moderator of an effect helps establish the boundary conditions of the effect (e.g., 
large vs. small, present vs. absent; Hayes, 2022; Kenny, 2018). Therefore, moderation analyses 
were an appropriate way to test whether self-efficacy related to cutting down on drinking had 
different effects on the relationship between SDoH and the likelihood of perceiving reductions in 
alcohol consumption. 
A logistic regression model with a moderator was fit using the PROCESS macro in 
SPSS. Continuous variables (i.e., SDoH adversity and self-efficacy) were mean-centered (Aiken 

26 
 
& West, 1991). While the overall model was significant, χ2(9) = 62.169, p < .001, and 
Nagelkerke's pseudo-R-squared = .502 indicates a moderate fit, the moderation term was not 
significant, indicating that self-efficacy did not moderate the relationship between adversity and 
perceived reductions in alcohol consumption, see Table 3 for full results. The model was rerun 
with only recovery identity as a covariate, excluding non-significant covariates. Again, while the 
overall model was significant, χ2(4) = 48.030, p < .001, and Nagelkerke's pseudo-R-squared = 
.403 indicates a moderate fit, the moderation term was not significant; see Table 4 for full 
results. Overall, these results suggest that self-efficacy does not significantly moderate the 
relationship between SDoH adversity and perceived reduced alcohol consumption. Therefore, the 
interaction term was removed for analysis of direct effects. 
The logistic regression model was rerun without the interaction term and was significant, 
χ2(10) = 57.172, p < .001, and Nagelkerke's pseudo-R-squared = .468 indicates a moderate fit. 
Regression coefficients estimated the effect of SDoH adversity on perceived decreases in alcohol 
consumption when accounting for self-efficacy. The regression coefficient of SDoH adversity 
was statistically significant and negative, supporting hypothesis one, B = -.229, SE = .121, 95% 
CI [-.467, -.096], indicating that as SDoH adversity increased, the odds of perceiving reduced 
alcohol consumption decreased (OR = 0.796). The estimated difference in SDoH adversity (selfefficacy held constant) and the likelihood of perceiving reduced alcohol consumption is higher 
than that of those with more SDoH adversity. Identifying as "in recovery" was associated with 
greater odds of perceiving reduced alcohol consumption (OR = 59.919), and previous substance 
use treatment was associated with lower odds of perceiving reduced alcohol consumption (OR = 
0.319), see Table 5 for full results. 
Discussion 

27 
 
 
This study aimed to test the moderating effects of self-efficacy for reducing alcohol use 
on the relation between social determinants of health (SDoH) adversity and perceived changes in 
alcohol use in a diverse sample of people who want to reduce their alcohol use. While theories 
have linked SDoH and self-efficacy (Swan et al., 2021; Witkiewitz et al., 2014), the literature has 
not considered interaction effects. This study tested individual and environmental factors using 
the social-ecological model and employed a comprehensive SDoH measure that covered all 
theorized domains, unlike prior studies. Because SDoH is multidimensional and interconnected, 
this comprehensive measure enhanced validity and was the first to examine its full impact on 
perceived change in alcohol use. This study's purposeful advertising and recruitment across 
diverse settings increased participant diversity, addressing typical homogeneity in substance use 
research and enhancing external validity. Its harm-reduction approach, endorsing recovery as a 
spectrum, further broadens its implications. 
When self-efficacy was accounted for, the regression coefficient of SDoH adversity was 
negative, supporting hypothesis one. This result indicates that individuals with more SDoH 
adversity were less likely to report reducing their alcohol consumption as SDoH adversity 
increases by one risk tally. Specifically, as SDoH adversity increased by one risk tally, 
participants were 44.32% less likely to report a decrease in their drinking, holding all else 
constant. This finding is consistent with previous research demonstrating the impact of the 
Neighborhood and Built Environment domain (Karriker-Jaffe et al., 2020) and SDoH adversity, 
as measured by latent profile analyses (Shokoohi et al., 2019; Swan et al., 2021), on reducing 
alcohol consumption. As this study is the first to include a validated measure of SDoH adversity, 
these results extend the body of research on the impact of SDoH on reduced alcohol 

28 
 
consumption, adding to the literature supporting the impact of community-level investment and 
infrastructure in addressing substance use. 
 
The interaction between SDoH adversity and self-efficacy was nonsignificant, which did 
not support hypothesis two, indicating that self-efficacy did not moderate the relationship 
between SDoH adversity and the likelihood of reporting that they reduced alcohol consumption. 
This is inconsistent with previous research that hypothesized greater self-efficacy for reducing 
alcohol use may be a point of intervention to buffer the effects of SDoH adversity on perceived 
drinking reductions (Kraus et al., 2016). This result could be due to heterogeneity in responses to 
the self-efficacy measure, including a small number of participants who identified as in recovery 
and reported that none or only a fraction of the alcohol-reduction strategies applied to them; 
therefore, they did not report confidence in using these strategies. This result could also be due to 
low power, as the final sample size was smaller than the a priori power analysis suggested; a post 
hoc power analysis estimated the achieved power to be 0.54. It can be difficult to detect a 
significant moderation effect, especially with a small sample size (Wang & Ware, 2014). It is 
possible that there is a significant moderation effect; however, there was not enough statistical 
power to reject the null hypothesis. 
There were no significant direct effects of self-efficacy on the likelihood of reporting 
reducing alcohol use. However, there was a weak negative correlation between self-efficacy and 
recovery identity, suggesting that identifying as "in recovery" was associated with lower selfefficacy scores. This finding may be due to participants who identify as "in recovery" not seeing 
themselves using the strategies listed in the measure and, as a result, reporting "lower" selfefficacy or indicating that the strategy "does not apply" to them. Participants reported the 
highest confidence in using the strategy of "Avoid catching up if you start drinking after others". 

29 
 
This suggests that participants are being mindful of drinking at their own pace without the 
urgency of "catching up" and avoiding drinking large amounts of alcohol in a short amount of 
time (Metrik et al., 2006; Sasso et al., 2022). Participants reported the lowest confidence in using 
the strategy "Keep track of each drink on your cell phone or piece of paper." Writing down 
drinks on a separate piece of paper or pulling out their phone to document what they drank may 
feel like extra effortful steps (Garnett et al., 2018; Milward et al., 2016); they may choose to 
prioritize another drinking reduction strategy instead. 
 
In addition to this study's hypotheses, there are other notable findings. Identifying as "in 
recovery" was associated with a 98% probability of participants reporting reducing their alcohol 
consumption. This finding is consistent with previous research demonstrating the relationship 
between recovery identity and reducing alcohol use (von Greiff & Skogens, 2021). This is also 
consistent with the theme within many definitions of recovery, of changing one's relationship 
with substances, including alcohol (Ashford et al., 2019; NIDA, n.d.; Zemore et al., 2023). 
Surprisingly, participants who reported previously engaging in substance use treatment 
were 24.20% less likely to report reducing their alcohol consumption. This may be due to the 
dichotomous measurement of previous substance use treatment, which may have overly 
simplified the treatment history. Substance use treatment varies across modalities, levels of care, 
and duration, and cannot be captured by a single dichotomous item. For example, an individual 
who has previously attended five episodes in a withdrawal management facility (e.g., "detox 
center") and one episode of a thirty-day intensive residential treatment program would be coded 
the same as someone who once attended a single intake session at an outpatient substance use 
clinic. It is not uncommon for individuals to attend substance use treatment multiple times, and 
the length of treatment is associated with maintaining recovery goals (Beaulieu et al., 2021). 

30 
 
Gender identity was not associated with the likelihood of reporting reducing alcohol 
consumption. This finding supports growing evidence of the gender gap closing in alcohol use 
(Goh et al., 2022; Kirsch et al., 2025; McCrady et al., 2020). Despite this closing, there is 
evidence that gender-specific and responsive treatment still needs to catch up to meet the needs 
of individuals with gender identities outside of "cisgender man" (Gilbert et al., 2019; Schamp et 
al., 2022), for which alcohol and substance use treatment has historically been developed and 
researched (Green, 2006). 
Second to identifying as a person of color, the most commonly endorsed "risk tally" 
among participants was employment (i.e., being unemployed, working part-time, or working 
temporarily). This reflects the increasing unemployment rate (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 
[BLS], 2026), low hiring rate (BLS, 2026), and growing concerns of adults in the United States 
(Gallup, 2026). The next most common "risk tally" was lack of social integration. This finding 
is consistent with the "loneliness epidemic" (Office of the Surgeon General, 2023) and recent 
publications highlighting the prevalence of loneliness or social isolation, about one in two United 
States adults (American Psychological Association, 2025; Bruss et al., 2024). The majority of the 
sample endorsed a high risk of SDoH adversity, and the average risk tally was higher than in 
previous community samples (Luzius et al., 2022; Osei Bach et al., 2023). This demonstrates that 
the sample is likely to experience significant health disparities and to have diverse experiences of 
SDoH adversity. In addition, participants experiencing SDoH adversity may be drinking to cope, 
and therefore may be less likely to reduce their alcohol use. 
Interestingly, SDoH adversity was moderately correlated with previous mutual aid 
engagement and previous substance use treatment. This association suggests that individuals 
experiencing SDoH adversity are likely to seek support for changing their alcohol use by 

31 
 
participating in mutual aid and substance use treatment. Previous research has documented that 
individuals who experience SDoH adversity (e.g., lower SES; lack of social support) attend 
mutual aid groups at higher rates than individuals with less adversity (Hai et al., 2022; Lookatch 
et al., 2020). In addition, previous mutual aid and previous substance use treatment were 
moderately correlated, indicating participants who engaged in one usually also engaged in the 
other. This is consistent with many substance use treatment programs encouraging participants to 
attend mutual aid groups for additional support, and mutual aid members also encourage 
participants to seek substance use treatment (Humphreys & Moos, 2007; Kelly & Yeterian, 
2011). 
Limitations and Future Directions 
It is important to consider the results in light of the following limitations. The 
heterogeneity of participants with "low self-efficacy" is a significant limitation of the present 
study, as it is possible that participants with "low self-efficacy" could include a mix of 
individuals who report low confidence in their ability to use alcohol reduction strategies and 
individuals who do not feel like many strategies apply to them, since they are in recovery or are 
abstinent from alcohol. Further, individuals with extended abstinence (i.e., years sober from 
alcohol) may not feel like they use the listed strategies, given their length of abstinence. It is 
possible that there may have been a significant interaction between SDoH adversity and selfefficacy if only individuals still consuming alcohol were included. Although this is a potential 
confound, the heterogeneity of participants with "low self-efficacy" highlights the diversity of 
experiences of individuals in recovery. Future research should continue to explore differences in 
experiences across the spectrum of recovery and harm reduction to better capture their 
experiences of "self-efficacy". 

32 
 
Unfortunately, the household size item was accidentally omitted from the survey; 
therefore, the federal poverty level (FPL) had to be determined for a household size of 1 person 
(i.e., the individual taking the survey). This is a limitation, as the mistake may have excluded 
individuals who qualify below FPL with a household size greater than 1, leading to an inaccurate 
count in their risk tally score. In addition, despite recruitment efforts, the final sample size was 
below the amount suggested by a priori power analysis, limiting power. Future research should 
consider incorporating branching logic and attention-check items to filter out potential fraudulent 
responses. 
Being a cross-sectional study limits the generalizability of the results. As a "snapshot" in 
time, results cannot infer causality. In addition, this study is limited by its reliance on 
participants' self-reports of whether they have reduced their alcohol consumption. Future studies 
should consider surveying participants at two time points to more accurately determine alcohol 
change and/or reductions in alcohol use. This study is also limited in its generalizability because, 
despite recruitment efforts, the sample was likely concentrated in a single geographic location 
(i.e., flyers hung only in the capital area of New York, social media recruitment on pages 
associated with the capital region of New York) and may have been affected by self-selection 
bias (Olsen, 2008). Because the survey uses an anonymous link, does not collect approximate 
location data, and lacks separate links to determine whether participants accessed the survey via 
online advertisements (e.g., listserv, social media) or a QR code on physical flyers, the exact 
extent of potential geographic bias cannot be determined. This limitation threatens external 
validity because the sample may exhibit less heterogeneity in SDoH due to potential geographic 
bias. Future research should seek to recruit participants from beyond a single local area to 
increase generalizability and reduce the likelihood of participant concentration in one area. 

33 
 
Additionally, although advertisements were displayed in a range of public spaces, this 
study was available only in English, which may have impacted the heterogeneity in SDoH. 
Specifically, research has found that English as the primary language spoken is associated with 
health literacy (Morris et al., 2006). This limitation may have prevented eligible individuals who 
are fluent in a language other than English from participating and contributed to the lowest "risk 
tally" endorsed, which was English literacy. Future research should validate alcohol use 
measures in languages other than English and actively offer the survey in those languages to 
capture heterogeneity in SDoH health literacy and improve generalizability by surveying a more 
diverse sample. 
Another limitation is the requirement that participants have access to an electronic device 
with internet access to complete the survey. This is a limitation because not all individuals in the 
target population may have an electronic device or one capable of accessing the survey 
(Andrade, 2020). This may also have affected the heterogeneity of SDoH adversity, as an 
individual's economic and housing status may limit their access to the internet. Previous research 
on access to the Internet and electronic devices among unhoused and low-income individuals has 
documented that most unhoused individuals own a smartphone and access the Internet daily via 
cellular data subscriptions or free public Wi-Fi (McInnes et al., 2013; Rhoades et al., 2017). 
Hence, to address the issue of accessing the survey online, flyers were posted in areas with free 
Wi-Fi (e.g., libraries). 
In addition, although theorized and supported by research on health disparities, measures 
of discrimination have historically not been included in SDoH measures, including PRAPARE. 
Omitting the impact of discrimination on health disparities undermines the internal validity of 
the construct measurement and the external validity of the built environment factor, as it does not 

34 
 
fully measure the construct theorized to include experiences of discrimination on health 
disparities. This omission threatens the accuracy of construct measurement and the applicability 
of the built environment factor in addressing health disparities. Specifically, marginalized 
identities are at higher risk of experiencing negative impacts of SDoH due to systemic 
oppression, which is why many SDoH measures, including PRAPARE, have an item on whether 
participants identify as POC, because the racial & ethnic identities are associated with a 
combination of social inequities present in systemic oppression (Macias-Konstantopoulos et al., 
2023; NACHC, 2021). Future research should work to include experiences of discrimination and 
validate a measure that fully measures the construct of the built environment domain. 
Clinical, Prevention, and Policy Implications 
Results of this study add to the literature supporting social policies that address SDoH 
adversity as part of alcohol use prevention (Karriker-Jaffe et al., 2024; Thornton et al., 2016). 
Specifically, this study demonstrates the significant association between SDoH adversity and 
reporting reducing alcohol use. Given the recent changes to social policies associated with 
SDoH, this result is timely, as many researchers anticipate negative impacts from reduced 
funding and from policies aimed at addressing SDoH inequities (Butler, 2026; Hamad, 2025). 
Based on the results of this study, it is reasonable to hypothesize that defunding programs that 
focus on addressing SDoH adversity will be associated with a lower likelihood of individuals 
reporting reducing their alcohol use. The results of this study highlight the importance of 
addressing SDoH adversity through policy changes and investments in programs that aim to 
reduce health disparities. 
Thus, the results emphasize the importance of environmental factors in reducing alcohol 
use and challenge the stigma of addiction as a choice, as the results indicate the significant 

35 
 
impact of SDoH adversity, often outside of an individual's control. Clinicians need to be aware 
of the significance of social-community factors to help de-stigmatize addiction and increase 
perceived access to recovery through offering psychoeducation to clients and families about 
SDoH adversity. Clinicians are uniquely positioned to validate clients' social and environmental 
factors that impact their substance use and to provide referrals to local resources and options that 
can help them succeed, including support for cultivating connection and community, as well as 
employment resources, assistance, and training. Finally, clinicians can advocate for healthcare 
changes that affect social determinants of health and alcohol use. Including speaking in support 
of policy initiatives, advocating for improved access to health information materials and services 
offered in languages other than English, and improving gender-responsiveness of substance use 
treatment.
